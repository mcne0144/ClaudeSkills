## Art Direction Techniques You Can Use (O_B_Ei5o1k4, 23 min)
- Thesis: art direction has repeatable tools, namely "ingredients" (visual decisions) that execute a big idea, and a good execution uses only two to three standout ingredients.
- Frameworks and named techniques:
  - Big idea pyramid: insight (unspoken human truth), then idea (creative formula, "the verb": what we do with the truth), then concept executed by the art director.
  - Art direction ingredients / menu: color, lighting, texture, depth of field, casting and talent, medium (photography vs illustration vs CGI), typography, music (non-visual but key). Menu is not exhaustive.
  - Spectrum from "visual feeling" (visuals support copy and make the idea felt, the look and feel) to "visual device / visual solution" (pairing of symbols becomes the message, little or no copy needed).
  - Visual device test: endless subjects can be slotted into it.
  - Chef metaphor: many ingredients, but two to three standout flavors; restraint. Art director as "visual orchestration" or one-man band.
  - Thread one ingredient across all touchpoints (social, out of home, pop-up/activation); ideally one that works in both an OOH ad and an IRL activation.
  - Music as ingredient: choose timeless, slightly nostalgic tracks; emotion sticks more than information.
- Examples:
  - Polaroid, "camera for an analog life" (copy lines like "AI can't generate sand between your toes"): ingredients are product photography and handwritten ballpoint pen lettering.
  - Oatly: big blocky type makes the copy memorable.
  - Diesel, "Enjoy before returning": flash photography (disposable-camera basement party feel), very good-looking models in an ordinary high school setting.
  - LEGO shadow ads: product plus a mismatched shadow (two blocks cast the shadow of a boat or dinosaur). Later evolved into kids building their own futures.
  - Sharp knife ad (brand [unsure spelling], not named): knife plus vegetables plus cutting board cut through.
  - Twitter (pre-X) hashtag campaign: hashtag symbol plus an icon or person representing a cultural topic.
  - Student spec work: Connor, Arc'teryx ("a play date with rain/wind/mud"; kids' coloring-book outlines, hand cut-out letters, close-up joyful photography; standout ingredient is the collaged cut-out letters). Ja [unsure spelling], an architect, EF Tours (boarding passes carrying "after I retire" style excuses; standout ingredient is the ticket as medium).
  - Musicbed artist Makeup and Vanity Set, songs "Solaris", "Plot" [unsure spelling], "Dogs".
- Method (implied by student examples):
  1. Write the insight (Arc'teryx: as we age we get worse at handling bad weather; avoiding discomfort makes us more sensitive).
  2. Write the idea as a verb phrase (reframe the elements as a playdate).
  3. Spend real time on 1 and 2 before executing (Connor said the first two weeks mattered most).
  4. Pick 2 to 3 standout ingredients that make the idea felt or that carry it.
  5. Thread them across every touchpoint.
- Quotes:
  - "There's usually like two to three standout flavors, standout ingredients."
  - "Imagining execution and the visual decisions we are making as ingredients helps me make this a more repeatable process."
- Claims worth verifying: none material. [his claim, unverified] students did these specs "in only 30 days".
- Tools/resources: Musicbed (sponsor, 14-day trial; skip pitch). His courses and live boot camps (paid, mentioned). No other tools.

## The Theory of Taste (gpXrRC3mGxU, 9 min)
- Thesis: passive taste is not a moat; the moat is what you can do with it, built by broadening inputs, learning to articulate taste, and going to the source.
- Frameworks and named techniques:
  - Three taste techniques: (1) broaden inputs, (2) express taste, (3) become visually literate.
  - "Visually cook" vs consume: taste metaphor is passive, art directors must use it.
  - Three levels of articulating taste: (a) know like/dislike (and reject deliberately); (b) express feeling, not technically, using references, similes, metaphors, collections titled by feeling ("uncomfy", "dream like", "tense", "Michel Gondry"); (c) name the ingredients (flash lighting, hand drawn mixed media effect, toasted oak). Pantry metaphor.
  - Feeling-level language gives clients a shared reference; ingredient-level language lets you brief executors, vendors and partners.
  - Trusted curators as a shortcut where you lack fluency (his example: music).
  - Immersion like language learning: go past the algorithm's one image to the full portfolio, notes and project descriptions.
  - Representation sites: find an artist's rep or agency, browse their other artists and image archives (clunkier search, but content not on Instagram or Pinterest).
- Examples: Michel Gondry (film feel); sushi chef moving to Japan; multilingual immersion; Musicbed "more human" curation.
- Steps:
  1. Save while scrolling using a share extension (Cosmos), turning escape time into an archive.
  2. When you save, ask why, and what specifically you like.
  3. Title collections by feeling.
  4. Break each into named ingredients and techniques.
  5. Click through to the source, find the photographer or director, view the full portfolio.
  6. Find their rep or agency and browse the roster and archives.
  7. Mood board becomes executable because you know who to call.
- Quotes:
  - "The moat is what you can do with it."
  - "You can't articulate taste you haven't actually experienced and stopped to collect."
- Claims worth verifying: [his claim, unverified] some stock music libraries have replaced human curation with AI.
- Tools/resources: Cosmos (with phone share extension); artist rep and agency websites. Sponsor: Musicbed (14-day trial); pitch skipped.

## The Lost Art of the Hook (in6uIQOfZWU, 12 min)
- Thesis: hooks long predate social media and the strongest ones come from a strong insight, not gimmicks; a hook grabs and holds.
- Frameworks and named techniques:
  - Hook attributes: (1) sparks curiosity, (2) reframes something familiar, (3) instantly understandable, (4) hits a nerve (funny, curious, emotional), (5) aligned with the story's idea so there is a payoff.
  - Fishing-hook logic: startling is not enough, you must hold.
  - Senses toolkit: visual (title/text, image) and audio (first line), plus one level deeper, the insight.
  - Insight: unspoken human truth, an aha or revelation, often counterintuitive, answers "why"; keep asking why. Insight vs observation is a spectrum (obvious truths are observations).
  - Chain: strong insight, then great idea, then great hook.
  - Music hook techniques: the mismatch (calm footage with aggressive track), the amplifier (music turns up the on-screen emotion), the sonic signature (repeat motifs across videos).
  - Music "hook" is the earworm, not necessarily the chorus; artists now put it at the start.
- Examples: openings of The Hunger Games, 1984 ("clocks were striking 13"), Fahrenheit 451, Red Rising, A Christmas Carol; Breaking Bad opening scene; The Dark Knight bank heist opening; book covers (Japanese wave cover) as thumbnail analog; newspaper headlines; ads "Think Small" (Volkswagen, brand not named in transcript), the Old Spice "The Man Your Man Could Smell Like" and "Winning isn't for everyone" [unsure which brand]; short film "Inside the Blind Iris" [unsure spelling]; his "lazy creative" series built on the insight that being lazy is a motivator to work smarter.
- Steps (hook check for a video idea):
  1. Does it spark curiosity?
  2. Can you reframe something familiar, make it counterintuitive?
  3. Is it clear and instantly understandable?
  4. Does it hit a nerve?
  5. Is the insight aligned with the content so there is a payoff?
- Quotes:
  - "A hook is what grabs and holds your attention."
  - "Great ideas come from great insights, and great ideas will in turn be great hooks."
- Claims worth verifying: [his claim, unverified] music industry has used the term "hook" for almost half a century. Creative director asked him "What's the hook?" early in his career (anecdote).
- Tools/resources: Musicbed mood filters (sponsor, 14-day trial; pitch skipped).

## How to Art Direct your Mood Board in Photoshop (uOvNil7caN4, 6.5 min)
- Thesis: a mood board is finished only after you art direct the references into a cohesive, one-author whole.
- Frameworks and named techniques:
  - Image selection test: does it contribute to the vision, reinforce tone, show a specific ingredient? If you cannot say why it is there, cut it.
  - Through line: board should feel made by one person with one intention.
  - Unifying layer: apply one shared treatment (color adjustment, LUT, or subtle grain) to all images.
  - Compose like a film: mix close-ups, wides and textures; vary activities and people; balance color and texture so nothing is lopsided.
  - Client-hat check: add text or voice over if anything could be misread, or remove the image.
- Examples: his sample board: earthy greens and browns, athletes and athletic apparel; a fluorescent yellow jacket recolored to olive.
- Steps (Photoshop):
  1. Bring image in; duplicate the layer (Cmd/Ctrl J), keep the original.
  2. Object Selection tool (W); in options bar choose People, pick the part (e.g. coat), Apply.
  3. In the contextual task bar, Add a mask.
  4. Adjust Colors (creates a Hue/Saturation layer); make it a clipping mask (Cmd Option G, or Alt-click between layers).
  5. Pick the detected color (yellow), shift hue (to green or olive), desaturate slightly.
  6. Add a Grain adjustment layer, kept subtle, to smooth quality differences across images.
  7. Copy Merged (Cmd Shift C), paste into the board (Firefly Boards or slide software); layers stay intact.
  8. Crop for what the viewer should see; arrange for balance.
- Quotes:
  - "I'm not the best art director in the world, but I pride myself in being a fast art director."
  - "If I can't answer why an image is there, it doesn't make the cut."
- Claims worth verifying: [his claim, unverified] Photoshop features described (Adjust Colors per-color targeting, Grain adjustment layer, device or cloud selection processing) are recent.
- Tools/resources: Adobe Photoshop (sponsor, paid partnership), Firefly Boards (in-browser collaborative concepting). Pitch skipped.

## How to Finish Every Project you Start (31MpSSV7Vuc, 6.5 min)
- Thesis: make the end of your guaranteed available time the finish line, and shrink scope to fit it.
- Frameworks and named techniques:
  - Time-based finish line: project is done when the timer ends, no other criteria.
  - Bad finish lines: "when it's perfect", "when it meets my standards", fixed page counts.
  - Guarantee test: if you cannot guarantee the time slot, shrink the scope.
  - Lower the stakes and get a cycle going (Rick Rubin idea): make many small things; each release makes the next easier.
  - Break large projects into episodes, series, collections.
- Examples: his one-hour-after-kids-asleep drawing posted to Instagram at minute 59; Rick Rubin, The Creative Act; Beeple (transcribed "people"), Everydays, daily art for 5,000 days.
- Steps:
  1. Identify the time you can truly guarantee.
  2. Set that as the finish line.
  3. Scope the project to fit (doodle not painting, comic strip not graphic novel, short GIF not film, short story not book).
  4. Or split big work into episodes or series.
  5. Stop and publish when time is up.
  6. Repeat, then package outputs into a larger portfolio piece.
- Seven ideas listed: doodle, comic strip, short GIF, short story, episodes instead of movies, series of interviews instead of documentary, collection of poems instead of novel.
- Quotes:
  - "Make the end of your guaranteed available time the finish line."
  - "Lower the stakes." (Rubin, read from the book; lines about a small work, a beginning)
- Claims worth verifying: [his claim, unverified] Beeple sold the collage for "50 million dollars" (public record is about $69 million, Christie's 2021; verify). Rubin quote wording read aloud, check against book.
- Tools/resources: none. No sponsor.

## How to Craft an Art Director Portfolio (GxKAwnIrQl4, 25 min)
- Thesis: A portfolio proves you think like an art director only if each project shows one strong idea stretched across touchpoints plus varied craft; employers will not hire what they cannot see.
- Frameworks and named techniques:

  - Multilingual art director: fluent enough in many visual languages (photo, editing, illustration, set design, color) to connect them under one idea.
  - Art direction menu: list of visual decisions that deliver a message emotionally (color and light, styling and set, cast and talent, visual references, mood). Pick a few "ingredients" per project with a purpose.
  - Big idea: overarching concept tying every touchpoint together; he separates size of idea from quality of idea.
  - Idea sizing chart (Next Art Director bootcamp): scale of ideas from business strategy down to tactics.
  - Conceptual art director: job-post label meaning someone who understands insights and turns them into big ideas.
  - Project checklist: named campaign, key visual or lockup, short overview with the why, 4 to 6 touchpoints, process/thinking notes.
  - Key visual or lockup: one image that distills the whole campaign concept.
  - Intention per project: each piece proves one thing (thinks big, craft, passion).
- Examples cited:
  - Nike "Rip the Script" (key visual: title plus cracked digital screen).
  - Hydro Flask "bottle scars" 360 campaign by a student ("because of three" in captions, [unsure spelling]): collaged print, social, stickers outlining scratches.

  4. Show 4 to 6 touchpoints across channels (print, out of home, social, activation, optionally video), each with a line tying it to the big idea.
  5. Include at least one 360 campaign; mix in craft, social, passion, and cultural-response projects.

## How to become an Art Director in 2026 (k6CHrvv7VFI, 24 min)
- Thesis: Art direction is concepts plus craft, is distinct from design, and can be learned on your own via insights, big ideas, and a spec portfolio of 4 to 6 strong projects.
- Frameworks and named techniques:
  - His definition: an art director creates and develops concepts and defines the look and feel across channels, directing designers, photographers, filmmakers.
  - Design vs art direction: designers favor function and clarity; art directors favor attention and impact.
  - Sir John Hegarty's contrast (as quoted by Jason): designer = "architect of clarity", maximum effect; art director = "creative anarchist", maximum impact.
  - Creative pipeline: strategy and brief, then creative team ideation, then sell-in, then execution with specialists. Art director enters earlier than designers but is not automatically more senior; copywriter is a peer; creative director sits above both.
  - Multilingual art director (multiple visual lanes, bring in specialists for gaps).
  - Insight: a revelation or unspoken human truth. Idea: what you do with the insight so others feel it.
  - Idea sizing chart: business/strategy/positioning, branding, big ideas for platforms and campaigns, then one-offs and tactics. Big ideas fit on a sticky note.
  - Brand platform: idea rooted in a universal truth that spans campaigns.
  - Tip: write the big idea with verbs.
  - Road map: learn craft, learn to think conceptually, make spec work, passion projects, apply.
- Examples cited:
  - Snickers "You're not you when you're hungry" (hunger changes behavior).
  - Dove "Real Beauty" (women judge their looks more harshly than others do).
  - Nike "Winning Isn't for Everyone" (flip the hero narrative).
  - His own Venmo spec for a homeless shelter, later done for real.
  - Brands he worked on: Amazon in-house (Coca-Cola, Pepsi, Kraft Heinz), Lululemon, Brooks Running.
  - Resources: "Hey Whipple, Squeeze This" (Luke Sullivan), Mark Pollard, Julian Cole, Asa Hakim [unsure spelling] (deck making, bootcamp speaker), Jason Gold (creative director; OpenAI, Amazon brand innovation lab, Droga5, RGA).
- Steps (self-taught path):
  1. Learn craft, filling gaps in visual languages (photo, storytelling).
  2. Learn insights, then big ideas and platforms.
  3. Make spec campaigns; convert passion projects by stressing the why.
  4. Build 1 to 2 360 campaigns, 1 craft project, optional social/experimental, a passion project, optionally a stunt or cultural response.
  5. Total 4 to 6 strong projects, no weak ones.
  6. Apply for internships or junior/mid art director roles at agencies, design studios, social-first agencies, or in-house.
- Quotes:
  - "Inevitably, you're going to get paid for the kind of work you're already making."
  - "Art direction is not design."
- Claims worth verifying:
  - Art direction will be among the most in-demand skills of 2026 [his claim, unverified].
  - Ad agency model "seems to be crumbling" while in-house, creator brand demand grows [his claim, unverified].
  - Hegarty quote attribution [his claim, unverified].
  - Creative director is not an entry-level role [his opinion].
  - Bootcamp: four weeks, four classes, four office hours.
- Tools and resources: FigJam road map; Next Art Director bootcamp (self-promo, skipped); private Slack community in bootcamp.

## How to Pivot to Art Direction (with no experience) (EKaakEMWxwA, 30 min)

  8. Add conflict to a human truth: a counterintuitive tension makes the message resonate (Luke Sullivan).
