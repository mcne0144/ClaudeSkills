## Core teachings

### 1. Taste, as his definition evolved across This Is Taste

- **Ridd (E1):** "the taste is understanding what you think should exist, right? And then being able to repeatedly answer why. And then the craft is executing against that taste." The filter is "what should exist," not what's trending.
- **Soren Iverson (E2):** taste as calibrated boundaries and a mental rolodex of references gathered with intention, shared rather than hoarded ("The Guardian, not the gatekeeper").
- **Colin and Samir (E3):** taste as judgment of people. "the most important creative decision we'll ever make isn't what we make, it's who we choose to make it for." His companion essay, "I was wrong about taste": "Taste is not just what you make. It is what you choose to care about. It is who you make room for. It is what you are willing to lose money on because you believe the thing should exist."
- **Taste versus execution are separable.** Jenny Wen (designing Claude at Anthropic): execution is making "a bunch of like different options"; taste is choosing among "these like 12 options" and knowing "the decisions that matter in visual quality." Tommy: "If you're just someone with great taste and no ability to execute, you're just kind of a critic."
- **Training exercises he collects:** the mentor walk (say one thing you like and dislike about every house you pass); answer "why" repeatedly; when something goes viral and you don't see why, study it; find people better than you; keep one non-AI day a week.

### 2. The mood board is the brief (his AI-era method)

From his Luma partnership video (Aug 2026) and his agent videos:
- Collect things that feel similar and that you love, without explaining why yet (images, video, essays, audio).
- Sort them into buckets and **name the buckets** ("Instrument Panel," "poetry," "botanical mainframe"). Names can mash up art history and style labels.
- Look for the item someone else would say doesn't belong. "Your ability to describe why it does belong, that connectedness, that's where your individual taste is living."
- Hand the whole board to the AI as the creative brief, no text prompt. Then direct: keep, move away, narrow into a visual system.
- His summary: "The vision comes from me, but the velocity comes from the tool."
- Note how this mirrors Jason Swet's Cosmos collections named after ingredients (file 01). Two independent teachers arrived at the same practice: named reference buckets are where taste becomes usable.

### 3. Jamey Gannon's AI Creative Director framework (State of Play, Jun 2026)

The most directly usable framework in his catalog for AI image work. Jamey Gannon is a brand creative director who teaches an AI Creative Director course.
1. **Aim before you shoot.** Set three inputs: model, references, prompt. Her stated oversimplification of models: diffusion models (e.g. Midjourney) are aesthetic and strong on style but weak on following instructions; multimodal models (e.g. Nano Banana, GPT Image) follow instructions and handle realism and text.
2. **An image is worth a thousand words.** She is "very anti-prompt": lead with subject and style references, keep prompts short. "all of this latent data exists in these images already. So like the mood, the lighting, the color, the vibe."
3. **Nothing good arrives finished.** Reject the one-shot mindset; finish by hand in Photoshop.
4. **Work in steps.** One change per pass (remove the label text, then crop, then apply). Stacked edits make the model "lazy and confused."
5. **Use the right tool for the job.** Put the tiny logo on in Photoshop; switch models when one fails.
- Also: run client mood-board voting (she built a swipe-style like, dislike, skip tool) to build shared language and expose clients to directions they would never name. Her claim: "every time an AI image model improves, you can cut your prompt in like half."
- Her career line: "invisibility is worse than incompetence."

### 4. Calibrating edge (Soren Iverson, This Is Taste E2)

Soren runs Iverson Inc (clients per the episode include Duolingo, LinkedIn, Figma) and built his name on absurd UI memes.
- **The hot sauce scale:** "mild, medium, hot, fire, Diablo." "We start at like 15 and then we dial it back to like eight or nine." Set the client's outer bound first, then work inside it.
- **Permission to fail, then invert:** "What's the worst way we could solve this problem?" Then flip one bad idea into a good one.
- **Comedy rules:** "be real, not tragic. Don't stay too long. Don't hit the joke too hard."
- **Absurd with a compass:** PG-13 weird (the Weird Al zone), not unhinged for its own sake.
- **Say it without saying it:** a Japanese roofing ad shows chaos on a roof, then just the roof, and never lists the benefits.
- Tommy's summary of the method: "Take something familiar, push it until something breaks, and use the weird to say the real." Lineage he draws: Surrealism, absurdist theater, memes as "digital absurdism, a way to process collective anxiety through humor."

### 5. Divergence before convergence

- **The Tarantino process** (Sara Vienna, Metalab): "playing out the end of the movie via design as quickly as you can." Sketch the App Store page, the sizzle, even the celebrity post, before building. Expect 80 to 90% to fail; there is "a nugget of something."
- **Discovery code** (Rauno Freiberg of Vercel, via Tommy): "Spaghetti code is how real discoveries happen." Try the stupidest idea 100 times, then step back 10 iterations. Tommy: "Discovery code is now cheaper than plywood."
- **Volume:** the clay pot story; Lee Black: "volume does not mean complexity," "Iteration is about cycles and not layers." Mansoor (a Dubai designer who uses scripts he can't read as shape libraries): "90% being ugly. That is the process."
- **The real question of AI speed:** "are you using the new speed to explore more territory or just arriving at the same destination faster?"

### 6. His frameworks for the AI transition

- **Floor raisers vs ceiling breakers.** Floor raisers codify (design systems, Tailwind: "Here's a decent button. Please stop making bad ones"). Ceiling breakers scout experimental territory. "Both are design work."
- **Unconstrained vs material exploration.** Napkin, canvas and physical prototypes versus exploring in the real material (code). A 2x2 with the floor/ceiling axis that he admits he's "still kind of workshopping."
- **Regression to the mean.** Models drift toward the average (Karri Saarinen of Linear, via Tommy); "homogeneity is the new risk." Weber Wong (FLORA): "consistency was the default... Now, like randomness is the default."
- **The Worth-It Test** (his, from history of tech panics): a disruption earns "a qualified yes" only if it brings (1) new capability, (2) upside that diffuses beyond owners, (3) believable new career ladders. He thinks AI clears the first, the second is open, the third worries him most. "the panic is often real and the prescription is usually wrong and the transition is rarely fair by default."
- **The third lane** (for craft businesses): purists who withdraw mostly die; participants without principles built the attention economy; the third lane is "participating in the thing you want to change in order to change it." "It does not need more manifestos. It needs more survivors."
- **Harness over model.** "An agent harness is air traffic control for your AI workflow." Three jobs: carry context ("The model is stateless, but your harness is stateful"), enforce governance (brand guidelines, approvals), hold history. "you can't control the quality of the model, but you can control the quality of the system." His agent build has four layers: skills, memory, context, connections. "An agent is not a chatbot. It is a car."
- **Dated prediction (April 2026, 18 months):** a major design team will ship a product where more than half the canvas frames were designed by an agent, "And it won't look like slop."

### 7. Brand, community and distribution

- **Coherence over consistency** (Ramp's brand team, via Dive Radio): "We need to build a brand system that behaves like jazz." "Consistency says we always look this way, but coherency says we evolve and yet you still know it's us." This is the most transferable brand idea in his catalog for a multi-location client: stores can vary within a recognizable system.
- **Cultural campfires.** "The next shift in social isn't 'more content.' It's smaller cultural pockets." Platforms are hollowing out; discovery moves to on-topic communities "where taste, trust, and yappers gather." For companies: "championing, not controlling. You bring storytellers and kindling to the campfire, invest in them and let them do what they do best and get out of the way." He keeps his own Discord "intentionally anti growth." Kevin Espiritu, via Tommy: "If you take too much from the audience without giving enough value, you don't have an audience anymore."
- **Legibility test** (from his games essay): breakouts "make sense immediately" and "carry itself in a thumbnail or within the first 30 seconds of footage." "You no longer need an ad budget. You just need something that gets someone to stop scrolling."
- **Retro constraints as authored style:** "The limitation is actually forcing a style that feels intentionally authored." And handmade signals carry a premium now (Soren: "things that are clearly humanmade have like a different type of value in the AI era").
- **Building is easy, distribution is the game** (his caption, Sep 2026).

### 8. Work, speed and portfolios

- **Speed is about return on investment** (Sara Vienna): "It's not speed versus quality. It's how do you earn the space for quality by demonstrating momentum?" (Tommy's outro). "Kind not nice": say the real thing, hard on the work and kind to the people.
- **Automate toil, augment creativity** (Jenny Wen): automate what people hate in their jobs; augment what they love.
- **Playgrounds over case studies** for discovery: "You don't need a full product. You just need proof of good judgment." Post format: "here's what I built. Here's what I learned. And here are the tools I used."
- **Spec work is back** (his Sep 2026 take): unsolicited prototypes for a company you want to work with get people shortlisted, because "the prototype was never really for the company. It's proof of how you think."
- **Phantom competency:** ship past your current level with AI until "that borrowed ceiling becomes the real floor that you stand on."

## Format craft (templates Bright Matter can use)

1. **"Design decisions that don't matter"** (his 2021 to 2022 TikTok series): a binary question, each option with one piece of evidence, a one-line verdict, all in about 25 seconds. Example: "Should the okay button come before or after cancel? ... two is less than three. Put the okay button on the right." Adaptable as "Dealer decisions that don't matter" or "Marketing decisions that don't matter" for client education.
2. **Who designed it better?** Head-to-head comparison of two real products on one interaction (Apple vs Samsung alarm). Works for any category with two visible competitors.
3. **Reaction to bad examples** (r/badUIbattles): the most viewed format he's ever made. The hook is shared outrage plus expertise.
4. **Documentary formula** (This Is Taste): promise the technique, deliver the values ("It turns out the technique is the easy part. What actually makes him good at this is something deeper"). Open loops (the Colin and Samir freeze-frame held until the end). Family and staff as witnesses. Host as participant who gets critiqued. Chapter titles that are memorable phrases. Ends on one second-person action.
5. **Essay spine:** thesis, counter-evidence, synthesis. Dense receipts, then explicit caveats ("I can't precisely prove causation"), plus a myth-busting moment that signals rigor.
6. **The packaging bargain stated openly:** clicky titles are "the airplane noises while I try to deliver you the vegetables."
7. **Name the thing:** toolbenders, cultural campfires, floor raisers and ceiling breakers, the Worth-It Test, the third lane, phantom competency, discovery code. Named concepts travel; this is the same lesson as Jason Swet's named frameworks earning his highest save rates.
8. **Dated, falsifiable predictions** create accountability and return visits.

## Evidence check

- **Form validation stat (his 2022 TikTok).** He says showing errors after typing "increases form success rate by 22% and user satisfaction by 31%." The figures are real: Luke Wroblewski with Etre, "Inline Validation in Web Forms," A List Apart, September 2009 **[primary]**. But they compare the best inline-validation form against a form that validated only on submit, tested with 22 participants. They do not compare "during typing" with "after typing," which is how the video frames them. The direction of his advice matches the study (validating after a field is completed did best), but the numbers answer a different question.
- **"Contour bias" and the "Kiki effect"** (his rounded-corners video) refer to real research lines (preference for curved contours; the bouba/kiki sound-shape association), but he presents them as settling a button-style debate. Treat them as tendencies, not rules.
- **Dark gray vs pure black in dark mode:** he cites Google Material Design's recommendation of dark gray **[primary: Google guidance]**; the eye-strain claim is stated without a study.
- **His own survey** (UX Tools State of Prototyping, 1,478 designers, March 2026): AI coders 43.8%, dabblers 18.5%, holdouts 37%; satisfaction 7.39/10 for heavy AI coders vs 5.93/10 for designers who don't code with AI. These are his survey results **[creator's own survey, self-selected sample]**; he says himself he "can't precisely prove causation."
- **Third-party figures in his essays** (Figma's State of the Designer, HBR, METR, Stanford via Time, Gloria Mark's attention-span work) are as he stated them and not independently checked here. One conflicts internally: the Figma report's sample size appears as 96 in captions and 906 in a chapter title.
- **His 2026 TikTok captions** include claims about AI model news (new model releases, a model being pulled after regulation). Treat these as his commentary, not verified facts.
- **Values signal:** he says he turned down "almost $40,000" from Higgsfield over ads like "Higgsfield AI just ended 20 more creative jobs": "I'm not going to take money from a company that is turning labor displacement into some sort of edgy marketing." Shannon uses Higgsfield as a production tool; this is about their marketing tone, not the tool's capability, but it is worth knowing if Bright Matter's own messaging about AI and jobs ever comes up.

## How Atelier should use this study

1. **AI image and video work defaults to Jamey Gannon's five principles.** Aim first (model, references, prompt), references before long prompts, one change per pass, finish by hand, switch tools freely. Pick diffusion-style models for aesthetic exploration and multimodal models for instruction-following, realism and text.
2. **The mood board is the brief.** Build named buckets, defend the odd one out, then hand the board to the generator. Pair with Jason's written mood-board rule: name the buckets and write the boundaries.
3. **Calibrate edge before concepting.** Ask where the client sits on the hot sauce scale, start at 15, dial to 8 or 9. Use "worst way to solve this, then invert" for ideation, and the comedy rules for social humor.
4. **Diverge before converging.** Play out the launch first (the Tarantino process): the post, the billboard, the review, the local news story.
5. **For multi-location brands, design for coherence, not consistency:** a system each store can play within and still be recognized.
6. **For community strategy, think campfires:** small, on-topic, generous rooms, with the brand as champion rather than controller.
7. **Fight regression to the mean:** distinctive references, authored constraints (retro, limited palette, non-Latin shapes), handmade signals.
8. **For client content, borrow his formats:** "decisions that don't matter" explainers, "who did it better" comparisons, and named principles that earn saves.
9. **Run his Worth-It Test** before recommending an AI-driven change to a client's team, and be honest about who absorbs the cost.
