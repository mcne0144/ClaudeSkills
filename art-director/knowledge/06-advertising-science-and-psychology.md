# 06. Advertising Science and Buyer Psychology

Knowledge file for the Bright Matter art-direction and creative-strategy agent. Purpose: explain the science behind brands, ads, creative direction and buyer psychology, and give the agent the evidence it needs to push back on weak ideas, including Shannon's and the client's.

Last research pass: 2026-10-03.

## How to use this file

- Every claim carries an evidence tier. Use the tier when you argue.
  - **[peer-reviewed]**: published in a refereed journal; strongest when meta-analysed or replicated.
  - **[primary]**: the author's own book or paper, or the platform's own documentation. Authoritative for "what X says", not proof that X is true.
  - **[practitioner databank]**: large but non-random practitioner datasets (IPA Databank, award entries). Strong pattern evidence, selection-biased.
  - **[vendor claim]**: a company selling the measurement or the media. Report as a claim.
  - **[practitioner lore]**: widely repeated, weakly evidenced.
  - **[flag]**: source problem noted (forum, aggregator, single reviewer, internal inconsistency).
- Status labels: **Replicated**, **Robust with boundaries**, **Contested**, **Failed to replicate**, **Lore**.
- Blank beats plausible. If a figure is not here with a source, do not invent one.
- The "Art direction implication" line is the actionable bit. Lead with it in client conversations, back it with the evidence line.

## Ehrenberg-Bass Institute: How Brands Grow

### What it says
- Byron Sharp, *How Brands Grow* (2010) and Romaniuk and Sharp, *How Brands Grow Part 2* (2015, revised 2021) [primary]. Brands grow mainly by increasing **penetration** (more buyers), not by deepening loyalty among existing buyers.
- **Mental availability**: the probability a brand comes to mind in buying situations. **Physical availability**: how easy it is to notice and buy.
- Growth levers: broad reach, consistent **distinctive brand assets**, links to many **category entry points**, wide distribution.
- Most of any brand's buyers are **light buyers** who buy it rarely. Ehrenberg-Bass work argues the Pareto "80/20" is closer to "60/20" in real purchase data (Sharp, Romaniuk and Graham, "Marketing's 60/20 Pareto Law", Ehrenberg-Bass working paper) [primary, working paper].

### Evidence and status
- Built on decades of panel-data regularities (Dirichlet model, Andrew Ehrenberg's work) [peer-reviewed lineage]. **Robust with boundaries.**
- Boundaries: strongest in repeat-purchase, mature categories with stable shares. Less tested for new categories, subscriptions with lock-in, luxury and very small local markets. Critics (including Mark Ritson) accept the laws but reject "target everyone" as a universal rule.
- For auto: car buying is infrequent and high-consideration. Penetration logic still holds (most future buyers are not in market now), but the purchase cycle is years long, so memory has to be built and refreshed between purchases.

### Art direction implication
Design for the light buyer who barely notices you: make every execution instantly attributable to the brand within the first moments, and favor broad, consistent brand codes over niche cleverness that only fans decode.

## Double jeopardy

### What it says
- Smaller brands have fewer buyers, and those buyers are also slightly less loyal. Big brands win on both counts. Ehrenberg, Goodhardt and Barwise, "Double Jeopardy Revisited", *Journal of Marketing* (1990) [peer-reviewed].
- Ehrenberg-Bass and the LinkedIn B2B Institute published a report arguing the law also holds in B2B ("The Double Jeopardy Law in B2B shows the way to grow") [primary, sponsored].

### Evidence and status
- **Replicated** across many categories and countries for decades. One of the most solid empirical generalizations in marketing.

### Art direction implication
A "loyalty campaign" for a challenger dealership rarely moves share. Creative should recruit new buyers. When a client asks for "content for our loyal fans", reframe it as content that also works for people who have never bought.

## Distinctive brand assets and the Distinctive Asset Grid

### What it says
- Jenni Romaniuk, *Building Distinctive Brand Assets* (2018) [primary]. A distinctive asset is a non-name cue (color, logo, character, shape, sound, tagline, celebrity) that triggers the brand.
- Two metrics [primary, Romaniuk interview in Contagious]:
  - **Fame**: share of category buyers who link the asset to the brand when shown the asset without the name.
  - **Uniqueness**: how much the brand owns the asset versus competitors.
- The **Distinctive Asset Grid** plots assets on fame (x) and uniqueness (y). Quadrants (book, ch. 11): "use or lose" (high both), "avoid solo use" (famous but shared), "investment potential" (unique but not yet famous), "ignore or test" (low both). A practitioner summary attributes a 50% threshold on each axis to the book [flag: secondary summary, check the book before quoting the threshold].
- Romaniuk on process: consistency of **form** matters most while building an asset; once strong, you can play with it. She prefers "meaning-free" assets whose strongest meaning becomes the brand. Assets should not be "fashion items" [primary, Contagious interview].

### Evidence and status
- The measurement approach is well documented and used commercially. **Robust as method.** Evidence that strong assets help: Nelson-Field/Amplified work found well-branded ads outperform even in low-attention placements [vendor claim, see Attention section].

### Art direction implication
Before any rebrand or "refresh", audit assets on fame and uniqueness. Never drop a high-fame, high-uniqueness asset for taste reasons. Build new assets by repeating one fixed form for a long time; resist designers varying the color, crop or character every campaign.

## Category entry points (CEPs)

### What it says
- CEPs are "the cues that category buyers use to access their memories when faced with a buying situation", internal (motives, emotions) or external (location, time) [primary: Romaniuk, Ehrenberg-Bass/LinkedIn B2B Institute, "Category Entry Points in a B2B World", 2022].
- Prioritize CEPs by the "3 Cs": credibility, competitiveness, commonality, leaving a "long shortlist" of five to eight [primary, Romaniuk via Marketing Week, 2022].
- Mental availability metrics: **mental market share** (brand share of all CEP-brand links), **mental penetration** (share of buyers linking the brand to at least one CEP), **network size** (average links among those who link) [primary].
- Romaniuk: top-of-mind awareness is not a valid measure of mental availability; "They should stop" [primary, Marketing Week].

### Evidence and status
- **Robust as a measurement and planning framework**; the causal claim that building CEP links grows sales rests on Ehrenberg-Bass memory-network theory and case evidence.

### Art direction implication
Brief creative around buying situations, not brand attributes. For a dealer group: "my lease is ending", "the check-engine light is on", "a new driver in the house", "tax refund season", "it's snowing and my tires are bald". Each CEP becomes a scene the ad shows, with the brand present in it.

## Binet and Field: The Long and the Short of It and the IPA Databank

### What it says
- Les Binet and Peter Field, *The Long and the Short of It* (IPA, 2013), based on the IPA Effectiveness Awards Databank [practitioner databank]. Thinkbox's summary of the report: the databank covers more than 700 brands in over 80 categories over 30 years [primary summary].
- Two kinds of marketing: **sales activation** (short-term, rational, targeted, response) and **brand building** (long-term, emotional, broad reach, builds memory and pricing power). You need both.
- Headline findings as summarized by Thinkbox: long-term (3+ years) campaigns deliver about double the profit of short-term ones; emotional advertising is about twice as efficient as rational and delivers about twice the profit; highly creative advertising is most effective but needs scale and time [primary summary].
- Earlier IPA analysis (Pringle and Field, *Brand Immortality*, 2008): campaigns with purely emotional content produced large profit gains about 31% of the time vs about 16% for purely rational [practitioner databank, figures via secondary summary; flag: verify against the book before quoting].
- Fame campaigns: Binet conference slides state "Fame increases efficiency x 4" [practitioner databank, slide deck].
- Brand-response campaigns (a brand idea that also drives action) perform near-optimally across both time frames [primary: Binet and Field, The Long and the Short of It].

### The 60/40 rule and its variations
- General B2C optimum: about 60% brand building, 40% activation [practitioner databank].
- *Effectiveness in Context* (IPA, 2018): the all-context sweet spot is 62:38, and it "varies by sector"; optimum brand share peaks in financial services and is lowest in perishable services such as travel [primary]. In high-research categories (financial services, durables including cars, subscriptions) campaign efficiency is about twice that of low-research categories, and the optimal balance tilts further toward brand [primary]. Online brands need more brand than offline ones; actual online-brand practice averaged about 56% brand, short of optimum [primary].
- Rule of thumb from the report: invest more in brand where activation is easy; invest more in activation where brand building is easy [primary, Field presentation].
- **B2B**: Binet and Field for the LinkedIn B2B Institute, *The 5 Principles of Growth in B2B Marketing* (2019): efficiency maximized at about 46% brand, 54% activation; the Institute rounds this to "50/50" [primary, sponsored by LinkedIn]. Based on IPA cases, of which only a small number are pure B2B [flag: small B2B sample, noted by Binet and Field themselves as early data].

### Evidence and status
- **Practitioner databank, consistent across many reports.** Not randomized. Selection bias: cases are award entries, so they skew toward success. Defenders note that comparing winners with non-winning entrants still isolates what works [IPA blog review, 2018].
- The ratios are averages across categories, not prescriptions for a single dealer.

### Art direction implication
Split the creative system into two jobs with different rules. Brand pieces: emotional, story-led, broad, consistent, judged on memory and long-term share. Activation pieces: offer, model, price, urgency, judged on response. For auto (a high-research durable), argue for a stronger brand share than the dealer's instinct, and make activation ads carry the same distinctive assets so they borrow brand memory.

## Share of voice and ESOV

### What it says
- **Excess share of voice (ESOV)** = share of voice minus share of market. Brands with positive ESOV tend to grow share; negative ESOV tends to shrink it.
- Binet and Field's widely cited rule of thumb (IPA, *Marketing in the Era of Accountability*, 2007): about 0.5 points of annual share growth per 10 points of ESOV, on average [practitioner databank; exact coefficient varies by category].
- The LinkedIn B2B Institute states that in B2B, "brands that set their share of voice above their share of market tend to grow" [primary, sponsored].
- *Effectiveness in Context* reports ESOV efficiency differs by context, roughly doubling in high-research categories [primary].

### Evidence and status
- **Consistent pattern in databank and Nielsen-style analyses.** Correlational. Works best where media spend is measurable; local markets with heavy organic and dealer co-op activity are noisier.

### Art direction implication
When budgets are small, creative must work harder per impression: distinctiveness, emotion and fame are how a low-ESOV brand punches above its weight.

## Les Binet and Peter Field: later work worth knowing

- *Media in Focus* (IPA, 2017): media roles for brand vs activation [primary].
- *Effectiveness in Context* (IPA, 2018): context modifies the rules (above) [primary].
- *The 5 Principles of Growth in B2B Marketing* (LinkedIn B2B Institute, 2019): B2B uses the same principles, with more activation [primary, sponsored].
- Peter Field, *The Crisis in Creative Effectiveness* (IPA, 2019): nearly 600 cases 1996 to 2018; creatively awarded campaigns had become no more effective than non-awarded ones, at their lowest effectiveness in 24 years of data, blamed on short-termism; "creativity has been hijacked for activation"; the gulf between good and bad creative practice shown as an 8x effectiveness gap [primary].
- Les Binet and Sarah Carter, *How Not to Plan* (2018): 66 practical rules [primary].

### Art direction implication
Awards-chasing stunts that run for six weeks are the pattern Field found losing effectiveness. Propose creative platforms that can run for years, with executions that refresh inside a fixed idea.

## Creative quality as the biggest effectiveness lever

### What it says
- NCSolutions, *Five Keys to Advertising Effectiveness* (2023): meta-study of nearly 450 CPG campaigns across TV and digital; creative drives 49% of incremental sales, brand 21%, reach 14%, targeting 11%, recency 5% [vendor claim, presented at ARF]. Creative's share was unchanged from the 2017 Nielsen/NCS study [vendor claim].
- The 2017 NCS/Nielsen report noted creative's share was higher in digital (56%) than TV (37%) in that sample, and that when a campaign was on target, media mattered more [vendor claim].
- Marketing Charts summary of the 2023 study: creative 48% in linear TV, 49% in digital [secondary].

### Evidence and status
- **Vendor meta-analyses, CPG-only, short-term sales lift.** Consistent direction across Nielsen, NCS and other vendors, but the method (machine-learning attribution of variance) is not peer-reviewed. Westwood One (a media seller) amplifies it [vendor claim, second-hand].

### Art direction implication
When a client wants to cut creative development to buy more media, the best available evidence says creative quality usually moves sales more than targeting does. Use it, label it as an industry meta-analysis of CPG, and note it measures short-term lift.

## System1 and Orlando Wood: Lemon and Look Out

### What it says
- Orlando Wood, *Lemon: How the Advertising Brain Turned Sour* (IPA, 2019) and *Look Out* (IPA, 2021) [primary]. Draws on Iain McGilchrist's hemisphere theory (*The Master and His Emissary*): the hemispheres "do things differently", not different things.
- **Right-brain features** associated with broad, long-term effects: living characters with agency, dialogue, a sense of place, music with melody, humour, a connected story, human uniqueness, movement, colour [primary, IPA/System1].
- **Left-brain features** associated with narrower, short-term effects: disembodied voiceover, rhythmic rapid cuts, abstraction, obtrusive on-screen text, product-as-hero isolation [primary].
- Look Out distinguishes "broad-beam" attention (brand building) from "narrow-beam" attention (activation) [primary, IPA press release].
- Evidence base: System1's Ad Ratings database of tested ads (System1 cited 30,000+ at the time of Lemon) linked to IPA effectiveness cases [vendor claim].
- System1 scores: **Star rating** (1 to 5.9 stars, predicting long-term brand growth from emotional response) and **Spike** (short-term sales potential, based on emotion plus brand recognition speed) [vendor claim].

### Evidence and status
- **Vendor-led research published by the IPA.** Peter Field endorses it [primary quote]. The neuroscience framing (hemisphere lateralization applied to ad features) is not settled science; the ad-feature correlations are System1's own analysis. Treat as a well-reasoned, partially validated practitioner model.

### Art direction implication
For brand films and long-form social: cast characters who talk to each other in a real place, use melody, let scenes breathe, and avoid the "product spinning on a gradient with a voiceover and flashing text" template. Keep left-brain devices for activation cutdowns.

## Kahneman: System 1 and System 2

### What it says
- Daniel Kahneman, *Thinking, Fast and Slow* (2011) [primary]. System 1 is fast, automatic, associative; System 2 is slow, effortful, deliberate. Most ad exposure is processed by System 1, at low attention.
- Related: Robert Heath, *The Hidden Power of Advertising* (2001), low-involvement processing: ads can build associations without active attention [primary].

### Evidence and status
- The dual-process framework is a **useful descriptive model**, widely used. Some studies cited in the book, notably the social-priming chapter, did not replicate; Kahneman publicly acknowledged he had placed too much faith in underpowered studies (2017 comment on Ulrich Schimmack's "Reconstruction of a Train Wreck" blog post) [primary statement].
- Heuristics-and-biases findings vary from rock-solid (anchoring, framing) to collapsed (several priming effects). Check each one below.

### Art direction implication
Design for the scrolling, half-watching System 1 viewer: one idea, instant brand recognition, low reading load. Do not cite "priming" studies to justify subliminal tricks.

## Behavioral economics biases for ads and pricing

### Anchoring
- **Says**: an initial number pulls later estimates toward it. Tversky and Kahneman (1974) [peer-reviewed].
- **Status**: **Replicated** strongly in the Many Labs 1 project (Klein et al., 2014, *Social Psychology*) [peer-reviewed]. Incidental anchors are weaker than explicit ones.
- **Art direction implication**: Show MSRP or the higher trim first, then the offer. In payment ads, the first number the eye lands on sets the frame, so control hierarchy deliberately.

### Decoy (attraction) effect
- **Says**: adding an asymmetrically dominated option increases choice of the option it most resembles. Huber, Payne and Puto (1982) [peer-reviewed].
- **Status**: **Contested**. Frederick, Lee and Baskin, "The Limits of Attraction", *Journal of Marketing Research* (2014): effects appeared with numeric attribute matrices but not when attributes were experienced or shown perceptually (photos), across 27 such studies [peer-reviewed]. Yang and Lynn (2014, JMR): only 11 of 91 attempts produced reliable effects [peer-reviewed]. Huber, Payne and Puto (2014) replied that the effect is robust under its original conditions [peer-reviewed commentary].
- **Art direction implication**: Decoys may work in numeric pricing tables (service plans, warranty tiers) and rarely in visual, experiential choices. Test before relying on them.

### Loss aversion
- **Says**: losses weigh more than equivalent gains. Kahneman and Tversky prospect theory (1979) [peer-reviewed].
- **Status**: **Robust**. Brown, Imai, Vieider and Camerer, *Journal of Economic Literature* (2024) meta-analysis of 607 estimates from 150 articles: mean loss-aversion coefficient 1.955, 95% interval 1.82 to 2.10 [peer-reviewed]. Gal and Rucker (2018, *Journal of Consumer Psychology*) argued loss aversion is less general than claimed [peer-reviewed critique].
- **Art direction implication**: "Don't lose your trade-in value" or "rates going up Friday" framing can outperform gain framing, but use it honestly; manufactured loss reads as pressure and erodes trust.

### Social proof
- **Says**: people follow what similar others do. Cialdini [primary].
- **Status**: **Replicated in the field with boundaries.** Goldstein, Cialdini and Griskevicius (2008, *Journal of Consumer Research*) hotel-towel norms study [peer-reviewed]; a German replication (Bohner and Schlüter, 2014, *PLoS ONE*) did not find the effect [peer-reviewed]. Allcott (2011, *Journal of Public Economics*), Opower home energy reports using neighbor comparisons, found durable reductions across very large samples [peer-reviewed].
- **Art direction implication**: Real review counts, star ratings, customer faces and "X families in [town] bought here" work. Specificity (local, similar people) matters more than big vague numbers.

### Scarcity
- **Says**: limited availability increases desirability.
- **Status**: **Meta-analysed, moderate, moderator-dependent.** Barton, Zlatevska and Oppewal (2022, *Journal of Retailing*), 416 effect sizes from 131 studies: demand-based scarcity works best for utilitarian products, supply-based for experiences, time-based for high-involvement products [peer-reviewed]. Ladeira et al. (2023, *Psychology and Marketing*) found demand-driven scarcity stronger than supply-driven, and no difference between limited-time and limited-quantity cues [peer-reviewed].
- **Art direction implication**: For vehicles (high involvement), real time-bound incentives and "popular, few left" (demand-based) are the strongest versions. Fake countdown timers risk regulatory and trust damage.

### Framing
- **Says**: logically equivalent descriptions change choices ("90% fat-free" vs "10% fat"). Tversky and Kahneman (1981) [peer-reviewed].
- **Status**: **Replicated** (gain/loss framing replicated in Many Labs 1, 2014) [peer-reviewed].
- **Art direction implication**: Test payment-per-month vs total-savings framings; per-day reframes ("less than a coffee a day") are a framing tool, use with care for compliance.

### Peak-end rule
- **Says**: people judge experiences mostly by the peak moment and the end, neglecting duration. Fredrickson and Kahneman (1993) [peer-reviewed].
- **Status**: **Replicated.** Alaybek et al. (2022, *Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes*), meta-analysis of 174 effect sizes: peak-end effect large (r = 0.581), duration effect essentially nil [peer-reviewed].
- **Art direction implication**: Design the dealership delivery moment and the last five seconds of a video as the "end". In service journeys, invest in one memorable peak (the key handover) and a strong ending rather than uniform polish.

### Mere exposure
- **Says**: repeated exposure increases liking. Zajonc (1968) [peer-reviewed].
- **Status**: **Replicated with an inverted-U.** Bornstein (1989, *Psychological Bulletin*) meta-analysis of 208 experiments [peer-reviewed]; Montoya et al. (2017, *Psychological Bulletin*) found liking rises then falls with many exposures [peer-reviewed].
- **Art direction implication**: Consistent assets and repetition build liking; plan rotation of executions inside one consistent look to avoid the downslope.

### Processing fluency
- **Says**: things that are easier to perceive and process feel more true, more likeable, more familiar. Reber, Schwarz and Winkielman (2004, *Personality and Social Psychology Review*) [peer-reviewed].
- **Status**: **Robust** across many paradigms.
- **Art direction implication**: Legible type, high contrast, simple layouts and familiar brand codes raise liking and believability. Disfluency can be used deliberately to slow attention on a single line, but not for the whole ad.

### Von Restorff (isolation) effect
- **Says**: a distinctive item in a list is remembered better. Von Restorff (1933); reviewed by Hunt (1995, *Psychonomic Bulletin and Review*) [peer-reviewed].
- **Status**: **Replicated.**
- **Art direction implication**: In a category where every dealer uses red-white-blue sale graphics, the visually isolated ad wins memory. This is the scientific basis for distinctiveness.

### Zeigarnik effect
- **Says (popular version)**: unfinished tasks are remembered better, so cliffhangers aid memory.
- **Status**: **Failed to replicate for memory.** Ghibellini and Meier (2025, *Humanities and Social Sciences Communications*) meta-analysis: "We found no memory advantage for unfinished tasks but found a general tendency to resume tasks" (the Ovsiankina effect) [peer-reviewed].
- **Art direction implication**: Do not justify cliffhanger formats with "Zeigarnik memory". Open loops may still drive completion and resumption (Ovsiankina), which is what episodic series and "part 2" posts actually need.

### IKEA effect
- **Says**: people value things more when they helped build them. Norton, Mochon and Ariely (2012, *Journal of Consumer Psychology*) [peer-reviewed].
- **Status**: **Original lab support; limited large independent replication.** Treat as plausible, not proven at scale.
- **Art direction implication**: Online "build and price" configurators and co-created content may raise valuation. Test it rather than promise it.

### Endowment effect
- **Says**: people value what they own more than identical items they do not. Kahneman, Knetsch and Thaler (1990, *Journal of Political Economy*) [peer-reviewed].
- **Status**: **Robust but contested on mechanism.** Plott and Zeiler (2005, *American Economic Review*) argued procedures drive some results [peer-reviewed].
- **Art direction implication**: Test drives, take-home trials and "picture yourself in it" imagery plausibly create a sense of ownership; trade-in owners overvalue their car, so trade-in messaging should validate before negotiating.

### Choice overload
- **Says**: too many options reduce choosing and satisfaction. Iyengar and Lepper (2000) jam study [peer-reviewed].
- **Status**: **Contested.** Scheibehenne, Greifeneder and Todd (2010, *Journal of Consumer Research*), 63 conditions, N = 5,036: mean effect "virtually zero" with high variance [peer-reviewed]. Chernev, Böckenholt and Goodman (2015, *Journal of Consumer Psychology*), 99 observations, N = 7,202: overload appears reliably when choice sets are complex, tasks difficult, preferences uncertain, or the goal is to minimize effort [peer-reviewed].
- **Art direction implication**: Do not strip inventory pages on folk wisdom. Simplify when the buyer is uncertain and the comparison is hard (finance options, trims); provide filters and a recommended default rather than fewer cars.

## Cialdini's seven principles of persuasion

### What it says
- Robert Cialdini, *Influence* (1984; new and expanded edition 2021) and *Pre-Suasion* (2016) [primary]: **reciprocity, commitment and consistency, social proof, authority, liking, scarcity, unity** (unity added in 2016).

### Evidence and status
- **Mixed, principle by principle.** Each principle draws on real experiments, but effect sizes vary and some flagship demonstrations have had replication problems (see social proof above). Foot-in-the-door and door-in-the-face techniques have meta-analytic support with small effects [peer-reviewed lineage]. Authority and liking are well supported in the persuasion literature. Unity (shared identity) is newer and less tested.

### Art direction implication
Use the principles as a checklist for an ad or landing page: Is there a genuine gift (reciprocity)? A small first yes (commitment)? Local proof? Credible authority (certified technicians, awards with dates)? Likeable people? Real scarcity? A "people like us" identity cue (unity, e.g., hometown)? Never fabricate any of them.

## Rory Sutherland: Alchemy and costly signalling

### What it says
- Rory Sutherland, *Alchemy* (2019) [primary]: much of marketing's value is psychological, not logical; small perceived changes can beat big rational ones; "the opposite of a good idea can also be a good idea".
- **Costly signalling**: expensive, visibly wasteful effort signals confidence and quality. Rooted in Zahavi's handicap principle (1975) and Spence's job-market signalling (1973) [peer-reviewed]. Advertising applications: Kirmani and Wright (1989, *Journal of Consumer Research*), perceived ad expense raises expected quality [peer-reviewed]; Ambler and Hollier (2004, *Journal of Advertising Research*), "The waste in advertising is the part that works" [peer-reviewed].

### Evidence and status
- The signalling mechanism has **peer-reviewed support**. Sutherland's specific anecdotes are **practitioner lore**: illuminating, not tested.

### Art direction implication
Production value, consistency over years, and visible investment (a real location, a real crowd, a premium print piece) signal a dealer is stable and trustworthy. Cheap-looking templated ads signal the opposite, even if they test fine on clicks.

## Segmentation and targeting: Sharp vs Kotler, and Mark Ritson

### What it says
- Kotler tradition: segment, target, position (STP); focus on the most valuable segment [primary textbook tradition].
- Sharp: competing brands sell to very similar customer bases; argue for "sophisticated mass marketing". Supported by Hammond, Ehrenberg and Goodhardt (1996, *European Journal of Marketing*), "Market segmentation for competitive brands" [peer-reviewed].
- Mark Ritson (Marketing Week columns, Mini MBA): accepts Ehrenberg-Bass laws but calls for "bothism": broad brand reach and targeted activation; strategy starts with diagnosis (research), then strategy (targeting, positioning, objectives), then tactics [practitioner].

### Evidence and status
- **Both sides are partly right.** User profiles of rival brands are usually similar (peer-reviewed). Targeting still matters for activation and for category definition (who buys the category at all). Ritson's synthesis aligns with Binet and Field's brand/activation split.

### Art direction implication
For brand work, cast and depict the broad category buyer; for activation, tailor creative to buying situations and in-market signals. Do not let "our customer is a 34-year-old female professional" narrow the brand film.

## Jobs to Be Done and the forces of progress

### What it says
- Clayton Christensen et al., "Know Your Customers' Jobs to Be Done" (*Harvard Business Review*, 2016) and *Competing Against Luck* (2016) [primary]: people "hire" products to make progress in a circumstance.
- Bob Moesta, *Demand-Side Sales 101* (2020) [primary]: four **forces of progress**: **push** of the current situation, **pull** of the new solution, **anxiety** about the new, **habit** of the present. Switching happens when push plus pull outweigh anxiety plus habit.

### Evidence and status
- **Practitioner framework**, built on interviews and case studies (the milkshake story). No controlled trials. Useful and widely used; not evidence in itself.

### Art direction implication
Map each force for a buyer: push ("my car keeps failing"), pull ("safety tech for my kid"), anxiety ("I'll get ripped off on trade"), habit ("I always fix the old one"). Assign creative pieces to each: proof and transparency ads reduce anxiety; ease and process ads reduce habit.

## Maslow's hierarchy: a critique

### What it says
- Maslow (1943, *Psychological Review*) [peer-reviewed]: needs in a hierarchy from physiological to self-actualization.

### Evidence and status
- **Contested.** Wahba and Bridwell (1976, *Organizational Behavior and Human Performance*) reviewed the evidence and found little support for the ranked hierarchy [peer-reviewed]. Later cross-cultural work (Tay and Diener, 2011, *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*) found universal needs but not a strict order [peer-reviewed].

### Art direction implication
Do not use the pyramid to argue "first sell safety, then status". Buyers hold several motives at once; use CEPs and jobs instead.

## Brand archetypes (Mark and Pearson)

### What it says
- Margaret Mark and Carol Pearson, *The Hero and the Outlaw* (2001) [primary]: twelve Jungian archetypes (Hero, Outlaw, Caregiver, Sage, etc.) as brand identities.

### Evidence and status
- **Lore.** Jungian archetypes lack strong empirical validation; we found no robust peer-reviewed evidence that archetype choice predicts brand performance. Ehrenberg-Bass's finding that buyers perceive little differentiation between brands (below) undercuts the idea that buyers hold rich archetypal brand images.

### Art direction implication
Use archetypes only as internal shorthand to keep tone consistent across a team. Never present them to a client as a performance driver.

## Distinctiveness vs differentiation

### What it says
- Ehrenberg-Bass: buyers perceive surprisingly little differentiation among competing brands. Romaniuk, Sharp and Ehrenberg (2007, *Australasian Marketing Journal*), "Evidence concerning the importance of perceived brand differentiation" [peer-reviewed]. Their prescription: be **distinctive** (easy to recognize) rather than chasing a unique meaning nobody notices.
- Counter-position: meaningful difference still matters for price premium and choice when buyers are evaluating (Ritson, Kantar's "Meaningful, Different, Salient" model) [practitioner; vendor claim for Kantar].

### Evidence and status
- **Distinctiveness evidence is stronger**; differentiation still matters when a real, communicable difference exists (a lifetime powertrain warranty, a no-haggle model).

### Art direction implication
Always build distinctiveness. Add differentiation only when the client has a genuine, provable difference, and then make that difference itself a distinctive asset (a name, a symbol, a recurring line).

## Semiotics in advertising

### What it says
- Saussure: a sign = **signifier** (form) + **signified** (concept) [primary].
- Roland Barthes, *Mythologies* (1957) and "Rhetoric of the Image" (1964, the Panzani pasta ad): ads work through denotation (literal) and connotation (cultural meaning, "Italianicity") [primary].
- Raymond Williams, *Marxism and Literature* (1977): cultural forms are **residual** (old), **dominant** (current norm), **emergent** (new) [primary]. Commercial semioticians map category codes onto this.

### Evidence and status
- **Interpretive method, not experimental science.** Valuable for decoding category conventions; outputs depend on analyst skill.

### Art direction implication
Map a category's codes: dealer advertising's dominant code (shouting presenters, starbursts, flag motifs), residual code (the founder in a showroom), emergent code (creator-style handheld walkarounds, quiet premium minimalism). Choose deliberately: borrow dominant codes when you need instant category recognition, break them for distinctiveness.

## Color psychology: honest evidence status

### What it says (popular)
- "Red means urgency, blue means trust", "color drives most snap judgments".

### Evidence and status
- Elliot and Maier, "Color Psychology", *Annual Review of Psychology* (2014): color can affect affect, cognition and behavior, but the literature is "at a nascent stage" and "considerable work on boundary conditions, moderators, and real-world generalizability is needed before strong conceptual statements and recommendations for application are warranted" [peer-reviewed].
- Elliot (2015, *Frontiers in Psychology*): much research is underpowered and lacks proper color control [peer-reviewed].
- Labrecque and Milne (2012, *Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science*), "Exciting red and competent blue": hue, saturation and value link to perceived brand personality [peer-reviewed, single program].
- The frequently quoted "62% to 90% of snap judgments are based on color" traces to a literature review (Singh, 2006, *Management Decision*) rather than an experiment [flag: weak basis, do not quote as fact].
- **Status**: **Nascent.** Context-dependent; color meaning is learned and category-specific.

### Art direction implication
Choose color for **distinctiveness and ownability** in the category (Romaniuk), and for legibility and contrast, not for claimed universal emotions. If a client cites "blue builds trust", answer: the evidence is too thin to bet on; owning a color the competitors do not use is the evidence-backed reason to choose one.

## Attention economics (Karen Nelson-Field)

### What it says
- Karen Nelson-Field, *The Attention Economy and How Media Works* (Palgrave, 2020) [primary]; founder of Amplified Intelligence [vendor].
- Measures **active attention** (eyes on ad), **passive attention** (ad on screen and in periphery, eyes not on it), **non-attention** using gaze tracking and face detection.
- Claims:
  - Active attention and short-term sales lift are significantly related; no attention means the ad cannot work; memory "starts to kick in at around the 3 second mark" (2020 DACH presentation) [vendor claim].
  - With a soft-drink brand: smaller brands got about four times more brand uplift per active second than larger brands; only 42% of branded moments were actively viewed (WARC, 2023) [vendor claim].
  - With Pinterest: passive attention was the top predictor of outcomes for big brands, active attention for small brands (2024) [vendor claim, platform-sponsored].
  - With VCCP (2025): 1.5 seconds of active attention can encode memory if distinctive assets are used well; "85% of placements receive less than 2.5 seconds" [vendor and agency claim].

### Evidence and status
- **Vendor research with real measurement**, partly published via WARC; several studies co-funded by platforms. Directionally consistent: attention is scarce, branding must land fast, distinctive assets make short exposures count.

### Art direction implication
Put the brand on screen early and repeatedly in ways that survive a 1 to 2 second glance (color field, logo, character), and make sure the branded moment coincides with peak visual interest, not a static end card.

## Fame and talkability

### What it says
- IPA databank: "fame" campaigns (that get talked about, become culturally salient) are among the most efficient; Binet slide: fame increases efficiency about 4x [practitioner databank].
- The Long and the Short of It: campaigns creating very large salience effects were almost twice as likely to deliver both top short-term sales growth and long-term share growth [practitioner databank].

### Evidence and status
- **Practitioner databank, consistent.** Word of mouth's role is plausible and widely measured by vendors; causal isolation is hard.

### Art direction implication
For a dealer group, fame means a recognizable local character, a recurring stunt, a line people repeat. Design for screenshot-ability and quote-ability.

## Consistency and wearout

### What it says
- Kantar ad tracking (2024): across about 1,600 tracked ads in three markets, "fed up" scores did not rise with frequency; true creative wearout of TV ads was rare, but digital ads showed higher "fed up" scores and dips in enjoyment, especially in a second burst [vendor claim].
- Kantar (2023): comparing second to first TV bursts, about a third of ads improved, a third stayed flat, a third declined; only 6% of digital ads held enjoyability unchanged on a second burst; campaigns that continue existing ideas score stronger on branding [vendor claim].
- ABX for the Radio Advertising Bureau: no wearout in 10 of 12 heavily spent radio ads [vendor claim, media-sponsored].
- Blair (1987, reprinted *Journal of Advertising Research* 2000): ARS data showed wear-out does occur for persuasion with repetition [peer-reviewed, older, vendor data].

### Evidence and status
- **Mixed, mostly vendor.** Consensus: consistent campaign ideas build branding; individual executions can fatigue, faster in digital feeds. Marketers tire of ads before audiences do (practitioner lore with some support).

### Art direction implication
Keep the idea and assets fixed for years; refresh executions on a schedule in feed channels. In paid social, fatigue is real and fast (see file 07 on platform fatigue signals), so plan variant libraries inside one consistent system.

## Humor and emotion effectiveness

### What it says
- Eisend (2009, *Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science*), meta-analysis of humor in advertising: humor increases attention, positive affect and attitude toward the ad, but lowers source credibility; effects on purchase intention are smaller [peer-reviewed].
- IPA databank: emotional campaigns outperform rational ones on long-term profit (see Binet and Field) [practitioner databank].
- System1: humour is a right-brain feature linked to long-term effects, and its use has declined [vendor claim].

### Evidence and status
- **Peer-reviewed for humor's attention and liking effects; practitioner databank for emotion and profit.**

### Art direction implication
Humor and warmth are strong for brand-building and attention; keep credibility cues (real staff, real prices, real reviews) in activation where trust is the issue. Make sure the joke is about the brand or category, or it gets remembered without the brand.

## Neuromarketing: claims and limits

### What it says
- Vendors sell EEG, fMRI, biometrics and eye tracking as windows into "true" responses.
- Ariely and Berns, "Neuromarketing: the hope and hype of neuroimaging in business" (*Nature Reviews Neuroscience*, 2010) [peer-reviewed]: real but narrow potential; many claims overreach.
- Venkatraman et al. (2015, *Journal of Marketing Research*): fMRI activity in the ventral striatum explained additional variance in real-world ad elasticities beyond traditional measures [peer-reviewed]. Falk, Berkman and Lieberman (2012, *Psychological Science*): neural responses of a small group predicted population-level response to anti-smoking ads [peer-reviewed].
- Poldrack (2006, *Trends in Cognitive Sciences*) on reverse inference: inferring a specific mental state from activation in a region is usually invalid [peer-reviewed].

### Evidence and status
- **Narrow validated uses; much hype.** Eye tracking and attention measurement are the most practical. "Buy button" and "subconscious desire" claims are not supported.

### Art direction implication
Use eye tracking or attention tools to check whether the brand and key message are seen. Reject vendor claims that a brain scan proves an ad "triggers desire".

## Persuasion and response models

### AIDA
- **Says**: Attention, Interest, Desire, Action; attributed to E. St. Elmo Lewis (around 1898) [primary attribution].
- **Status**: **Lore as a causal model**; useful as a copy checklist.

### Hierarchy of effects
- **Says**: buyers move through cognitive, affective, then conative stages. Lavidge and Steiner (1961, *Journal of Marketing*) [peer-reviewed].
- **Status**: **Not well supported as a fixed sequence.** Vakratsas and Ambler (1999, *Journal of Marketing*), "How Advertising Works: What Do We Really Know?": little support for a fixed hierarchy; effects depend on context, product and involvement [peer-reviewed].

### Elaboration Likelihood Model (ELM)
- **Says**: high motivation and ability lead to the central route (argument quality matters); low leads to the peripheral route (cues like attractiveness, number of arguments, music). Petty and Cacioppo (1986) [primary]; Petty, Cacioppo and Schumann (1983, *Journal of Consumer Research*) razor ad experiment [peer-reviewed].
- **Status**: **Widely supported**, with debates about whether two routes are distinct (Kruglanski's unimodel). Good practical guide.

### Art direction implication
Most scroll exposure is peripheral-route: rely on distinctive cues, faces, music and simple claims. Product detail pages, VDPs and comparison content are central-route: give strong, specific arguments, specs, prices and proof. Do not force one creative to do both jobs.

## Common client claims and the evidence-based reply

| Client says | Reply with |
|---|---|
| "Let's target only our best customers." | Double jeopardy and penetration; light buyers are most of future sales. |
| "Change the look, it's getting stale." | Distinctive asset fame/uniqueness audit; marketers tire before buyers (Kantar). |
| "Red creates urgency." | Elliot and Maier: evidence nascent; choose color for ownability. |
| "Fewer choices sell more." | Choice overload meta-analyses: depends on difficulty and uncertainty. |
| "Cliffhangers stick in memory (Zeigarnik)." | 2025 meta-analysis found no memory advantage. |
| "Just run offers, brand is fluff." | Binet and Field; for high-research durables, optimal brand share is higher. |
| "Neuro test proves it works." | Reverse inference problem; ask for validated outcome links. |
| "We need a hero archetype." | Lore; use CEPs and assets instead. |
