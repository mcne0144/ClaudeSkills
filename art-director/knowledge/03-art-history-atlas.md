# 03 World Art History Atlas: Ancient to Now

Reference atlas for the Bright Matter art-direction agent. Chronological, deliberately global. Each entry uses the same fields so the agent can search and compare:

- **When / where**: era and place
- **Visual DNA**: 2-4 defining traits in technical vocabulary (composition, color, line, space, light, material)
- **Key artists / works**
- **Idea**: the worldview that produced the look
- **Use it today**: a concrete way to borrow it in a brand, campaign, avatar, or 3D concept
- **Lineage**: where a current ad, design, or social trend descends from it (when there is a clear line)

Working rules for the agent:

- Borrow the *principle* (a compositional logic, a color system, a way of treating space) before borrowing the *surface* (motifs, symbols). Principles travel; surfaces can be appropriation.
- Living cultural traditions (Aboriginal Australian, Maori, Pacific, Indigenous American, sacred Islamic and Hindu imagery) carry ownership and protocol. Recommend collaboration, licensing, and credit with artists from that community instead of imitation.
- When challenging a brief, pull a reference from at least two different regions or millennia. A "minimal" brief can be answered with Agnes Martin *or* a Joseon moon jar *or* a Zen ink circle; each says something different about minimalism.
- Dates here are given only where well documented. Absence of a date is deliberate.

# PART 1: DEEP TIME AND THE ANCIENT WORLD

## Prehistoric Cave and Rock Art (Sulawesi, Chauvet, Lascaux)

- **When / where**: Maros-Pangkep karst, Sulawesi, Indonesia: a narrative scene at Leang Karampuang (human-like figures interacting with a pig) dated to at least 51,200 years ago (Nature, 2024), the oldest securely dated figurative and narrative art known. Chauvet Cave, France (Aurignacian). Lascaux, Dordogne, France (Upper Paleolithic, Magdalenian era, roughly 17,000 years ago). Also Altamira (Spain), Bhimbetka (India), Arnhem Land (Australia), Serra da Capivara (Brazil), Laas Geel (Somaliland).
- **Visual DNA**:
  - Profile silhouettes in red and yellow ochre, manganese and charcoal black; contour line carries the volume.
  - Rock surface used as found relief: bulges become haunches, cracks become ground lines (an early form of site-specific, "2.5D" work).
  - Hand stencils made by blowing pigment around the hand: the first negative-space mark and the first signature.
  - Overlapping, unframed composition; animals float without horizon, implying motion when seen by flickering lamplight.
- **Key works**: Leang Karampuang pig scene; Leang Tedongnge warty pig (at least 45,500 years); Lascaux Hall of the Bulls; Chauvet lions and horses panel.
- **Idea**: Image as presence and story. Sulawesi pushes the origin of visual storytelling to Southeast Asia, not Europe. Images likely carried myth, hunting knowledge, and identity across generations.
- **Use it today**: Hand-stencil motif as a community-brand mark (every customer's "handprint" as user-generated content); ochre-and-charcoal palette with rough mineral texture for an outdoor gear or natural-skincare brand; projection-mapped animal silhouettes on raw concrete for an immersive launch event, lit to flicker like firelight.
- **Lineage**: The hand-stencil logic survives in protest stencils and Banksy-style spray work; "earthy mineral" palettes in wellness branding descend loosely from ochre.

## Ancient Egypt

- **When / where**: Nile valley, roughly 3100 BCE (unification) to the Roman period; Old, Middle, New Kingdom peaks.
- **Visual DNA**:
  - Composite (aspective) view: head and legs in profile, eye and shoulders frontal; the body shown in its most legible parts rather than from one viewpoint.
  - Canon of proportion on a square grid (an 18-square standing figure in classic periods): image making as a modular system.
  - Hierarchical scale (the pharaoh largest), register bands as horizontal reading lines, hieroglyphs integrated with image.
  - Flat, unmodulated mineral color: Egyptian blue (the first synthetic pigment), red ochre, malachite green, gold for divine flesh.
- **Key works**: Narmer Palette; Bust of Nefertiti (Amarna period, Thutmose workshop); Tomb of Nebamun wall paintings (British Museum); Tutankhamun's gold mask.
- **Idea**: Art as permanence (ma'at, cosmic order). Images were made to function eternally for the dead, so clarity and completeness beat momentary appearance. Amarna under Akhenaten is the notable break toward curvilinear, intimate naturalism.
- **Use it today**: Register-band layouts for a long-scroll landing page or carousel (each band a "story strip"); composite-view illustration for an avatar system where every character reads instantly in silhouette; Egyptian-blue plus gold as a luxury fragrance palette.
- **Lineage**: Modular proportion grids anticipate design-system tokens; Egyptomania surged after the 1922 Tutankhamun discovery and fed directly into Art Deco ornament.

## Mesopotamia (Sumer, Akkad, Assyria, Babylon)

- **When / where**: Tigris-Euphrates region (modern Iraq, Syria), roughly 3500 BCE to 539 BCE.
- **Visual DNA**:
  - Cylinder seals: rolled intaglio making a continuous repeating frieze, effectively the first infinite-scroll pattern and a proto-printing method.
  - Low-relief narrative in stacked registers; strong stylization of hair, beards, and musculature as decorative pattern.
  - Glazed brick in saturated blue with gold-yellow animals in relief.
  - Monumental guardian hybrids (lamassu: human head, bull or lion body, wings) designed with five legs so they read correctly from front and side.
- **Key works**: Standard of Ur (British Museum); Stele of Naram-Sin; Assyrian lion-hunt reliefs from Nineveh (British Museum); Ishtar Gate of Babylon, built under Nebuchadnezzar II (Pergamon Museum, Berlin).
- **Idea**: Kingship, divine order, and administration. Writing (cuneiform) and image grow up together as instruments of power and record.
- **Use it today**: The cylinder-seal "rolling repeat" as a brand pattern generator (a single motif tile that rolls into ribbons for packaging tape and web borders); the lamassu two-view trick for 3D mascots meant to be seen from a front camera and a profile camera.
- **Lineage**: Glazed-brick blue and stylized animals echo in Art Deco and in many Middle Eastern contemporary identities.

## Indus Valley (Harappan) Civilization

- **When / where**: Indus basin, modern Pakistan and northwest India, mature phase roughly 2600-1900 BCE (Mohenjo-daro, Harappa, Dholavira).
- **Visual DNA**:
  - Steatite stamp seals: tiny square formats with a single dominant animal (often a "unicorn"-like bull) above a line of undeciphered script; an ancient logo lockup of mark plus wordmark.
  - Grid-planned cities, standardized bricks and weights: design as civic standardization.
  - Small-scale lost-wax bronze with confident, relaxed posture.
- **Key works**: "Dancing Girl" bronze from Mohenjo-daro; "Priest-King" bust; unicorn seals.
- **Idea**: Order, trade, and standardization without monumental royal propaganda; an unusually egalitarian-seeming material culture (interpretation still debated).
- **Use it today**: The seal as the template for a compact square brand mark (symbol over a short line of type) suited to app icons and social avatars; standardized-module storytelling for a logistics or fintech brand.

## Ancient China: Bronzes, Calligraphy, and Shan Shui

- **When / where**: Shang and Zhou bronzes (2nd to 1st millennium BCE); Sanxingdui (Sichuan) bronze culture; calligraphy from oracle bones to the classic scripts; landscape painting (shan shui, "mountain-water") maturing in the Five Dynasties and Northern Song.
- **Visual DNA**:
  - **Bronzes**: piece-mold casting; taotie mask motif built from bilaterally symmetrical zoomorphic parts on a leiwen (thunder-spiral) ground; flanges that articulate edges like architectural ribs.
  - **Calligraphy**: line as the record of a body in time; brush pressure, speed, dry brush ("flying white"), and the balance of ink to empty paper. Scripts run from seal (zhuanshu) to clerical (lishu), regular (kaishu), running (xingshu), and cursive (caoshu).
  - **Shan shui**: monumental vertical composition, mist as negative space, shifting multiple viewpoints instead of a single vanishing point. Guo Xi's "three distances" (high, deep, level) structure the space.
  - Xie He's Six Principles, led by qiyun shengdong ("spirit resonance, life-motion"): vitality over likeness.
- **Key artists / works**: Shang ritual vessels (ding, gu); Sanxingdui bronze masks with protruding eyes; Wang Xizhi, *Preface to the Orchid Pavilion Gathering* (known through copies); Fan Kuan, *Travelers among Mountains and Streams* (National Palace Museum, Taipei); Guo Xi, *Early Spring* (1072).
- **Idea**: Daoist and Confucian cosmology. The human is small within nature; painting is a "spirit journey" (woyou, wandering while lying down). Calligraphy reveals character and cultivation.
- **Use it today**: Shan shui vertical format for 9:16 social video: mist-gradient layers, tiny human figure, slow upward pan for a tea, spa, or travel brand; a calligraphic gesture as a dynamic logo signature that animates stroke by stroke; taotie bilateral symmetry for a gaming-avatar mask or a premium spirits seal.
- **Lineage**: Mist-layered parallax landscapes in game art and app onboarding; "brush-stroke" logos; Sanxingdui's uncanny masks are a frequent reference in contemporary Chinese sci-fi and fantasy character design.

## Ancient Greece

- **When / where**: Aegean world; Archaic (c. 700-480 BCE), Classical (480-323 BCE), Hellenistic (323-31 BCE).
- **Visual DNA**:
  - Canon of ideal proportion (Polykleitos) and contrapposto: weight shift creating an S-curve of relaxed, living balance.
  - Pottery in black-figure (black silhouettes, incised detail) then red-figure (figures left in clay red, background painted black; finer interior line).
  - Architectural orders (Doric, Ionic, Corinthian); optical corrections such as entasis on Parthenon columns.
  - Original polychromy: sculpture and buildings were painted, which the white-marble myth forgets.
- **Key works**: Kritios Boy; Polykleitos, *Doryphoros* (Roman copies); Parthenon sculptures (British Museum, Acropolis Museum); *Nike of Samothrace* and *Laocoön* (Hellenistic drama).
- **Idea**: The human as the measure; rational proportion as beauty; civic identity. Hellenistic art adds pathos, movement, and individual emotion.
- **Use it today**: Red-figure inversion (figure in warm terracotta reserved from a black field) for a Mediterranean restaurant's packaging or menu; contrapposto pose direction for avatar and model shoots that need "relaxed authority"; Hellenistic wind-blown drapery as a 3D cloth-sim reference.
- **Lineage**: The "statue meme" and vaporwave classical busts; fashion campaigns staging models as marble; the corrected "white marble" story is itself a useful brand narrative about truth and restoration.

## Ancient Rome

- **When / where**: Italy and the Mediterranean, Republic to Empire (roughly 509 BCE to 476 CE in the West).
- **Visual DNA**:
  - Verism: warts-and-all portraiture of elders as a sign of experience and virtue.
  - Wall painting in the four "Pompeian styles": faux marble, illusionistic architecture opening the wall, delicate ornament on flat color, and fantasy architecture.
  - Engineering as image: arch, vault, dome, concrete; the oculus of the Pantheon as a light instrument.
  - Narrative spiral relief (Trajan's Column) as a continuous storyboard.
- **Key works**: Villa of the Mysteries frieze (Pompeii), with its saturated "Pompeian red"; Pantheon; Trajan's Column; Fayum mummy portraits from Roman Egypt (encaustic on wood, startlingly modern).
- **Idea**: Empire, civic virtue, public works, and propaganda; mass-produced imagery (coins with the emperor's head) as brand distribution.
- **Use it today**: Fayum-portrait direction for an AI avatar set: frontal gaze, warm encaustic skin, dark ground, large eyes; Pompeian red as a hero color for a wine bar; Trajan's Column spiral as a scroll-driven web narrative.
- **Lineage**: Coins as the first mass brand-distribution medium; Trajan's Column inscription is the source of classic Roman capitals (see file 04, Trajan typeface).

## Mesoamerica and the Andes: Olmec, Maya, Aztec (Mexica), Inca

- **When / where**: Olmec Gulf Coast (2nd to 1st millennium BCE); Classic Maya lowlands (roughly 250-900 CE); Aztec Tenochtitlan (14th century to 1521); Inca Andes (15th century to the Spanish conquest in the 1530s). Earlier Andean textile cultures include Paracas and Nazca.
- **Visual DNA**:
  - **Olmec**: colossal basalt heads; jade carving; volume compressed into heavy, rounded mass.
  - **Maya**: elegant calligraphic line in murals and polychrome vessels; glyph blocks combining image and text in square cartouches; Maya blue (an indigo and palygorskite clay pigment of remarkable durability).
  - **Aztec**: dense, symmetrical, radially organized stone relief; fierce frontality; codices with flat color fields, black contour, and glyphic figures.
  - **Inca**: precisely fitted polygonal ashlar masonry with no mortar; tocapu, a geometric square-unit textile system; quipu knotted-cord records.
- **Key works**: Olmec colossal heads (San Lorenzo, La Venta); Bonampak murals; Palenque reliefs; Aztec Sun Stone (Museo Nacional de Antropología, Mexico City); Coyolxauhqui stone; Sacsayhuamán and Machu Picchu walls; Inca tocapu tunics (Dumbarton Oaks).
- **Idea**: Cyclical cosmic time, divine kingship, sacrifice and renewal (Mesoamerica); reciprocity, administration, and harmony with mountains (apus) in the Andes.
- **Use it today**: Maya glyph-block logic for a modular icon set in which each icon is a compact square of symbol plus mark; Inca polygonal masonry as a 3D brand environment (interlocking irregular solids for a construction or cybersecurity brand: "no gaps"); tocapu grid as a textile-inspired pattern system, ideally co-created with Andean weavers.
- **Lineage**: Chicano and Mexican muralism draw on Aztec and Maya imagery; "Aztec print" in fast fashion is a common appropriation; avoid it without collaboration.

## Nok, Ife, and Benin (West Africa)

- **When / where**: Nok culture, central Nigeria (terracotta production roughly 900 BCE onward per recent excavation; the Met dates the terracottas c. 500 BCE-200 CE). Ife (Ile-Ife), Yoruba, Nigeria, classic period roughly 12th-15th centuries. Kingdom of Benin (Edo), Nigeria, court art from the 13th-14th centuries onward; much looted by the British in the 1897 punitive expedition.
- **Visual DNA**:
  - **Nok**: hollow terracotta heads with elaborate coiffures; D-shaped or triangular eyes with pierced pupils; geometric stylization of features; burnished surfaces.
  - **Ife**: serene, idealized naturalism in copper alloy and terracotta; fine vertical striations on faces; perforations at the hairline and mouth possibly for attaching beads or veils.
  - **Benin**: lost-wax cast brass plaques in high relief with hierarchical scale; quatrefoil ground patterns; ivory masks (Queen Mother Idia); coral-bead regalia rendered in metal.
- **Key works**: Nok seated figures; Ife "Olokun" and crowned heads (British Museum, National Commission for Museums and Monuments, Nigeria); Benin plaques and the Idia ivory pendant mask (British Museum, Met).
- **Idea**: Sacred kingship, ancestor veneration, the head (ori in Yoruba thought) as the seat of destiny and identity. Benin plaques also documented court life and history: an archive in metal.
- **Use it today**: Ife's calm, idealized frontal portrait as direction for a dignified avatar or 3D heritage character (striation as a stylized surface detail, never a costume); Benin plaque layout (central figure, flanking attendants, patterned ground) for a team or founder portrait series; brass, coral, and dark wood as a material palette for a premium West African food or beauty brand built with West African creatives.
- **Lineage**: The Benin Bronzes restitution debate (Germany began returning objects to Nigeria in 2022) is now part of how these objects are discussed; any brand reference should acknowledge provenance.

## Islamic Art: Geometry, Arabesque, Calligraphy

- **When / where**: From the 7th century across the Umayyad, Abbasid, Fatimid, Seljuk, Nasrid, Timurid, Safavid, Ottoman, and Mughal worlds, from Spain to Central Asia and India.
- **Visual DNA**:
  - Geometric pattern built from compass and straightedge: star polygons, interlacing strapwork, girih tiles (a set of five tiles that can produce complex quasi-periodic patterns, as at the Darb-i Imam shrine, Isfahan).
  - Arabesque: endlessly branching vegetal scrolls with no start or end.
  - Muqarnas: honeycomb vaulting that fractures light into a cascade of small cells.
  - Calligraphy as primary art: angular Kufic, rounded Naskh, monumental Thuluth, Nastaliq in Persianate lands; square Kufic composed into brick and tile like pixel art.
- **Key works**: Dome of the Rock, Jerusalem (completed late 7th century); Great Mosque of Córdoba; Alhambra, Granada (Nasrid); Shah Mosque, Isfahan; Blue Qur'an (Kufic, gold on indigo parchment).
- **Idea**: Aniconism in religious contexts directs creativity to pattern and the written word; infinite pattern expresses the unity (tawhid) and infinity of God; geometry as sacred order.
- **Use it today**: A parametric girih generator as a living brand pattern that never repeats exactly across a campaign's assets; square Kufic logic for a pixel-grid wordmark; muqarnas as a 3D lighting study for a hospitality lobby or a premium product hero render. Avoid sacred texts as decoration.
- **Lineage**: Escher studied the Alhambra; quasi-crystal and parametric design in contemporary architecture (and generative art) connects back to girih; much "geometric luxury" branding draws from here.

## Byzantine Art

- **When / where**: Eastern Roman Empire centered on Constantinople, roughly 330-1453 CE; also Ravenna, Sinai, and Orthodox Russia and the Balkans after.
- **Visual DNA**:
  - Gold-ground mosaics with tesserae set at slight angles so the surface glitters and shifts as the viewer moves.
  - Frontal, hieratic figures with large eyes, elongated proportions, minimal modeling: presence over illusion.
  - Inverse (reverse) perspective in icons: lines diverge toward the back, so space projects toward the viewer.
  - Rich jewel color: lapis blue, porphyry purple, gold.
- **Key works**: Hagia Sophia, Constantinople (dedicated 537); San Vitale, Ravenna, Justinian and Theodora mosaics; Christ Pantocrator icon, St. Catherine's Monastery, Sinai; Andrei Rublev's *Trinity* (Russian).
- **Idea**: The image as window to the divine; icons are venerated as presences. The Iconoclast controversy (8th-9th centuries) is one of history's great arguments about what images are allowed to do.
- **Use it today**: Gold-ground "icon" portraits for a VIP loyalty tier or a luxury watch campaign; angled-tile shader in 3D for a glittering surface that changes with camera move; frontal hieratic avatar for a brand persona meant to feel authoritative and timeless.
- **Lineage**: Klimt's golden phase draws directly from Ravenna; "saint" iconography is a recurring fashion-campaign trope (Dolce and Gabbana, streetwear "saint" tees).

## Indian Art: Ajanta, Chola Bronzes, Mughal and Rajput Painting

- **When / where**: Ajanta caves, Maharashtra (2nd century BCE to roughly the 5th century CE); Chola bronzes, Tamil Nadu (9th-13th centuries); Mughal painting at court from Akbar (later 16th century) through Jahangir and Shah Jahan; Rajput painting in Rajasthan (Mewar, Bundi, Kishangarh) and Pahari hill states (Basohli, Guler, Kangra), 17th-19th centuries.
- **Visual DNA**:
  - **Ajanta**: flowing tempera murals; tribhanga (triple-bend) pose; dense crowded narratives of Jataka tales; warm earth palette with modeled flesh.
  - **Chola**: lost-wax bronze in dynamic equilibrium; Shiva Nataraja inside a ring of fire as a perfect circle-and-figure composition.
  - **Mughal**: Persian-trained precision fused with European naturalism; detailed portraiture, natural-history studies, ornate illuminated borders (hashiya); gold and fine squirrel-hair brush.
  - **Rajput / Pahari**: flat saturated grounds (saffron, vermilion, emerald), stylized almond eyes, emotional color (rasa), lyrical devotional themes (Krishna and Radha), architecture shown in tilted planes.
- **Key artists / works**: Ajanta Padmapani bodhisattva; Chola *Shiva Nataraja* (Met, Rijksmuseum, Chennai); the *Hamzanama* (Akbar's atelier); Abu'l Hasan and Ustad Mansur under Jahangir; Nihal Chand, *Bani Thani* (Kishangarh); Kangra *Gita Govinda* series.
- **Idea**: Devotion (bhakti), the aesthetic theory of rasa (savoring emotion), and courtly self-image. Mughal art is cosmopolitan synthesis; Rajput art is emotional intensity.
- **Use it today**: Rajput flat color fields and tilted architecture for a Diwali campaign or a modern Indian restaurant identity, with illustration by South Asian artists; Mughal hashiya border as a framing device for premium social posts; Nataraja circle composition for a dance, fitness, or energy brand mascot in motion.
- **Lineage**: Contemporary South Asian illustration (and the "modern miniature" movement led by artists like Shahzia Sikander, trained in miniature at the National College of Arts, Lahore) remixes these into editorial and brand work.

## Persian Miniature Painting

- **When / where**: Iran and Central Asia, roughly 13th-17th centuries; peaks at the Timurid court in Herat (late 15th century) and the Safavid court in Tabriz and Qazvin (16th century).
- **Visual DNA**:
  - Elevated, multi-point "bird's-eye" space with no single vanishing point; stacked planes so every figure is visible.
  - Jewel-like flat color (lapis, malachite, vermilion, gold), crisp contour, intricate patterned surfaces (tiles, carpets, robes).
  - Image and text share the page; compositions break out of their frames into the margin.
- **Key artists / works**: Kamal ud-Din Bihzad (Herat); Sultan Muhammad, *The Court of Gayumars* from the *Shahnama* of Shah Tahmasp (Aga Khan Museum); Reza Abbasi (Isfahan single-figure studies).
- **Idea**: Poetry made visible; a paradisiacal, ideal world (the garden as paradise). Clarity and abundance over illusionistic realism.
- **Use it today**: Frame-breaking composition for a social carousel where elements spill across slide borders; isometric-like stacked space for a "map of the brand world" illustration; jewel palette for a Nowruz or Ramadan campaign co-created with Iranian or Central Asian illustrators.
- **Lineage**: Matisse cited Persian art as a source for flat decorative color; isometric illustration in tech marketing shares the multi-plane logic.

## Japan: Heian, Rinpa, Ukiyo-e, Wabi-Sabi, Ma

- **When / where**: Heian period (794-1185) court culture; Rinpa school from the early 17th century (Kyoto) through Edo; ukiyo-e woodblock prints in Edo (Tokyo), 17th-19th centuries; wabi-sabi crystallized through the tea ceremony (Sen no Rikyu, 16th century); ma as a pervasive concept of interval.
- **Visual DNA**:
  - **Heian emaki (handscrolls)**: fukinuki yatai ("blown-off roof") overhead interior views; hikime kagibana (slit eyes, hook nose) faces giving anonymous, universal figures; tsukuri-e built-up opaque color; diagonal reading as the scroll unrolls.
  - **Rinpa**: gold- and silver-leaf grounds; bold, decorative, asymmetrical composition; tarashikomi (dropping ink or pigment into wet pigment for pooled, organic edges); repeated stencil-like motifs.
  - **Ukiyo-e**: flattened planes of color, strong keyline contour, dramatic cropping and diagonals, high or low viewpoint, bokashi gradation (gradient wiped on the block), Prussian blue in later prints.
  - **Wabi-sabi**: imperfection, asymmetry, patina, rough natural materials, muted earth tones; **ma**: meaningful emptiness, the interval that gives form its tension.
- **Key artists / works**: *Tale of Genji* handscrolls (12th century); Tawaraya Sotatsu, *Wind God and Thunder God* screens; Ogata Korin, *Irises* screens (Nezu Museum); Katsushika Hokusai, *Under the Wave off Kanagawa* (Great Wave) from *Thirty-six Views of Mount Fuji*; Utagawa Hiroshige, *One Hundred Famous Views of Edo*; Kitagawa Utamaro bijin-ga; raku tea bowls; kintsugi repair.
- **Idea**: Ukiyo means "floating world": fleeting urban pleasure, kabuki, courtesans, travel. Rinpa celebrates seasonal nature as decoration. Wabi-sabi and ma come out of Zen and tea: beauty in transience, incompleteness, and pause.
- **Use it today**: Ukiyo-e flattened planes and bold contour for a ramen brand's packaging system, each SKU a "view" of the same mountain; Rinpa gold-leaf asymmetry for a luxury hotel's seasonal campaign; ma as a layout rule for a skincare website (60%+ negative space, one object); kintsugi as a brand story for repair, resilience, or recommerce; Heian blown-off-roof view as a 3D cutaway of a store or home for an explainer.
- **Lineage**: Japonisme shaped Impressionism, Post-Impressionism (Van Gogh copied Hiroshige), and Art Nouveau; manga and anime flat-color cel shading; the "Japandi" interior trend; the Great Wave is among the most remixed images in advertising.

## Korea: Joseon White Porcelain and the Moon Jar

- **When / where**: Joseon dynasty (1392-1910). Moon jars (dalhangari, historically called daeho, "big jar") appear in the late 17th century and through the 18th century.
- **Visual DNA**:
  - Large white porcelain globe thrown as two hemispheres joined at the waist, so each jar has a slightly uneven, unique profile.
  - Milky, warm white glaze with kiln-induced color shifts and blemishes; undecorated surface.
  - Also from Joseon: buncheong stoneware with brushed white slip; chaekgeori (books-and-scholar's-objects) screens with reverse-perspective shelving; minhwa folk painting with bright, cheerful symbolism.
- **Key works**: Moon jars at the British Museum and the Met (second half 18th century); National Museum of Korea holdings.
- **Idea**: Neo-Confucian restraint, frugality, and integrity; the white of the scholar-official. Today the moon jar stands for a uniquely Korean aesthetic of natural, unforced imperfection (and was the inspiration for the 2018 PyeongChang Olympic cauldron, per the Met).
- **Use it today**: Moon-jar silhouette and warm-white palette for a K-beauty or tea brand: a single slightly irregular sphere as hero 3D object, soft diffuse light, matte glaze shader; chaekgeori shelving as a product-grid layout for an e-commerce homepage; minhwa tigers and magpies as a playful seasonal campaign illustrated by Korean artists.
- **Lineage**: "Imperfect" handmade ceramics as a lifestyle-brand prop; the global minimal-craft aesthetic owes much to Korean and Japanese ceramic traditions.

## Aboriginal Australian Art

- **When / where**: Continuous tradition across Australia for tens of thousands of years (rock art in Arnhem Land, Kimberley, Murujuga); the contemporary Western Desert acrylic movement began at Papunya in 1971, where teacher Geoffrey Bardon encouraged painting and Papunya Tula Artists formed.
- **Visual DNA**:
  - Aerial, map-like perspective: country seen from above, with concentric circles (sites, waterholes), tracks, and U-shapes (seated people).
  - Dotting (often a public overlay that can conceal restricted sacred detail), rarrk cross-hatching in Arnhem Land bark painting, and "x-ray" style rock art showing internal organs.
  - Earth and ochre palettes in traditional work; vibrant acrylic in contemporary practice.
- **Key artists / works**: Clifford Possum Tjapaltjarri, *Warlugulong*; Emily Kame Kngwarreye, *Earth's Creation*; Rover Thomas (Warmun); John Mawurndjul (rarrk bark painting); Gloria Petyarre.
- **Idea**: The Dreaming (Jukurrpa in Warlpiri, Tjukurpa in Anangu languages): ancestral law, story, and country are one. Imagery is owned by specific people and families; who may paint what is governed by law.
- **Use it today**: Do not imitate. Commission or license through Aboriginal-owned art centres, with artists' credit and royalties (Australian protocols such as those published by the Australia Council's Protocols for using First Nations Cultural and Intellectual Property guide this). Transferable principle without appropriation: aerial "map of relationships" storytelling for a community or place-based brand, drawn in the brand's own visual language.
- **Lineage**: Kngwarreye is often compared to Abstract Expressionism, but her work comes from a separate lineage; the comparison is Western framing. Fake "Aboriginal-style" souvenir art is a long-running case study in cultural harm.

## Polynesian and Pacific Art

- **When / where**: Across Polynesia (Hawai'i, Aotearoa New Zealand, Samoa, Tonga, Tahiti, Rapa Nui, Marquesas) and wider Oceania.
- **Visual DNA**:
  - Tapa (barkcloth: siapo in Samoa, ngatu in Tonga, kapa in Hawai'i): rubbed or stamped geometric patterns in brown, black, and red earth tones.
  - Tatau (Samoan) and ta moko (Maori): line-based body art where every motif carries genealogy, rank, and story.
  - Maori whakairo (carving) with spirals and the koru (unfurling fern frond); kowhaiwhai painted rafter patterns in red, black, and white.
  - Monumental stone figures (Rapa Nui moai) with strong brow, elongated face, and ancestral gaze.
- **Key works**: Rapa Nui moai (one, Hoa Hakananai'a, is in the British Museum and subject to return requests); Hawaiian feathered cloaks ('ahu'ula); carved Maori meeting houses (wharenui).
- **Idea**: Genealogy (whakapapa), mana, ancestral connection, navigation and the ocean as connector. Pattern is identity, not decoration.
- **Use it today**: Partner with Pacific artists and follow cultural advisers; never use moko or tatau motifs on avatars or products without permission. Transferable principle: a pattern system where each motif encodes meaning (a brand language where shapes stand for values or product lines) can be designed in the brand's own vocabulary.
- **Lineage**: Tribal tattoo trends of the 1990s are a cautionary example of meaning stripped from motif; Aotearoa New Zealand's contemporary national branding works with Maori designers.

# PART 2: MEDIEVAL TO 19TH CENTURY (EUROPE IN GLOBAL CONTEXT)

## Gothic

- **When / where**: Northern France from the mid-12th century (Abbey of Saint-Denis under Abbot Suger), spreading across Europe through the 15th century.
- **Visual DNA**:
  - Pointed arch, rib vault, and flying buttress allow thin walls and immense windows: verticality and dematerialized structure.
  - Stained glass: deep cobalt and ruby light (lux nova) turning architecture into colored atmosphere.
  - Elongated, swaying figures; illuminated manuscripts with gold leaf and marginalia (often comic or bizarre).
  - International Gothic: refined, courtly, decorative naturalism.
- **Key works**: Chartres Cathedral (glass, Royal Portal); Sainte-Chapelle, Paris; Limbourg brothers, *Très Riches Heures du Duc de Berry*; Giotto's Arena (Scrovegni) Chapel bridging to Renaissance naturalism.
- **Idea**: God as light; the cathedral as an image of the Heavenly Jerusalem; scholastic order made visible.
- **Use it today**: Stained-glass logic (black lead line plus saturated jewel fills) for an illustration system or a projection show; vertical, light-flooded 3D environment for a fragrance launch; marginalia-style doodles as a brand's playful secondary layer in editorial.
- **Lineage**: Blackletter type (see file 04); goth and metal subcultures; "dark academia" social aesthetic.

## Renaissance (Italian and Northern)

- **When / where**: Florence and Italy from the early 15th century, peaking in the High Renaissance (roughly 1490s-1520s); Netherlands, Flanders, Germany in parallel.
- **Visual DNA**:
  - Linear perspective with a single vanishing point (Brunelleschi's demonstrations; codified in Alberti's *De pictura*, 1435).
  - Chiaroscuro modeling, sfumato (Leonardo's smoky transitions), pyramidal composition.
  - Northern oil glazing: luminous, microscopically detailed surfaces and reflections (van Eyck).
  - Revival of classical proportion and architecture; idealized anatomy.
- **Key artists / works**: Masaccio, *Holy Trinity*; Leonardo da Vinci, *Mona Lisa*, *Last Supper*; Michelangelo, Sistine Chapel ceiling, *David*; Raphael, *School of Athens*; Jan van Eyck, *Arnolfini Portrait* (1434, National Gallery London); Albrecht Dürer's engravings and self-portraits.
- **Idea**: Humanism: the individual, reason, observation, and the rediscovery of antiquity, financed by merchant patronage (the Medici). Note the Renaissance was connected: its pigments, paper, and math came via Islamic and Asian trade and scholarship.
- **Use it today**: One-point perspective as a hero-shot composition for a product launch in a symmetrical corridor; sfumato lighting for portrait avatars (soft falloff, no hard edge); van Eyck convex-mirror reflection as a 3D easter-egg device in a product render.
- **Lineage**: The patron-artist model resembles brand-creator partnerships; Renaissance parody is an evergreen ad trope (the *Creation of Adam* hands, *Last Supper* tableaux).

## Mannerism (brief)

- **When / where**: Italy, roughly 1520-1600.
- **Visual DNA**: Elongated limbs, serpentine poses (figura serpentinata), acid and shot colors, compressed ambiguous space.
- **Key artists**: Pontormo, Parmigianino (*Madonna with the Long Neck*), Bronzino; El Greco carried it into Spain.
- **Idea**: After perfection, artifice: style itself becomes the subject.
- **Use it today**: Stretched proportions and acid-pastel palettes for fashion avatars that feel deliberately unreal.

## Baroque

- **When / where**: Rome from around 1600, spreading through Catholic Europe, Spain, Flanders, the Dutch Republic, and colonial Latin America (where it fused with Indigenous craft, e.g., Cusco School painting).
- **Visual DNA**:
  - Tenebrism: a hard raking spotlight out of near-black (Caravaggio).
  - Diagonal, dynamic composition and theatrical moments of maximum drama.
  - Illusionistic ceilings (quadratura), architecture and sculpture merged into total environments.
  - Dutch Golden Age variant: domestic interiors, still life, light through windows, and the open art market.
- **Key artists / works**: Caravaggio, *The Calling of Saint Matthew*; Gian Lorenzo Bernini, *Ecstasy of Saint Teresa*; Artemisia Gentileschi, *Judith Slaying Holofernes*; Rembrandt, *The Night Watch*; Diego Velázquez, *Las Meninas* (1656); Peter Paul Rubens; Johannes Vermeer, *Girl with a Pearl Earring*.
- **Idea**: Counter-Reformation persuasion: art to move the emotions and win hearts; in the Dutch Republic, merchant pride and the material world.
- **Use it today**: Single-source tenebrist lighting for a whisky, coffee, or watch campaign (key light at 45 degrees, no fill); Dutch still-life composition for food and beverage shoots (overflowing abundance plus a memento mori detail); Vermeer window light for warm lifestyle photography.
- **Lineage**: "Moody food photography," chiaroscuro beauty lighting, Netflix-style prestige drama grading; *Las Meninas* is the ancestor of every meta, "look at who's looking" ad.

## Rococo

- **When / where**: France, roughly 1720s-1770s, spreading to German palaces.
- **Visual DNA**: Pastel palettes (powder blue, pink, mint, ivory); asymmetric C- and S-scroll rocaille ornament; feathery brushwork; intimate scale, garden settings, flirtation.
- **Key artists / works**: Antoine Watteau (fête galante, *Pilgrimage to Cythera*); François Boucher; Jean-Honoré Fragonard, *The Swing* (Wallace Collection).
- **Idea**: Aristocratic pleasure and private intimacy after Louis XIV's grandeur; lightness as a lifestyle.
- **Use it today**: Pastel palette and swirling ornament for a patisserie, bridal, or candy brand; *The Swing* staging (lifted skirt, flying slipper, garden) as a playful fashion campaign homage.
- **Lineage**: Sofia Coppola's *Marie Antoinette* look; Ladurée-style packaging; "coquette" and "princesscore" social aesthetics.

## Neoclassicism

- **When / where**: Europe and the Americas, roughly 1760s-1830s, after the excavations of Herculaneum and Pompeii.
- **Visual DNA**: Crisp contour and smooth, enamel-like finish; frieze-like planar composition; austere, controlled palette; heroic classical subjects.
- **Key artists / works**: Jacques-Louis David, *Oath of the Horatii*, *Death of Marat*; Antonio Canova, *Psyche Revived by Cupid's Kiss*; Jean-Auguste-Dominique Ingres; Angelica Kauffman.
- **Idea**: Enlightenment reason and republican virtue in reaction to Rococo frivolity; art as moral instruction and political messaging.
- **Use it today**: Frieze composition (figures in a single shallow plane, side lit) for a law firm, bank, or civic campaign signaling integrity; marble-and-white for a premium dental or pharmacy brand; David's political portraiture logic for a founder or cause campaign.
- **Lineage**: Federal-style government buildings; the "classical serif plus white space" look of institutional branding.

## Romanticism

- **When / where**: Europe, roughly 1790s-1850s; with American Hudson River School as a cousin.
- **Visual DNA**: The sublime: vast nature dwarfing the figure; turbulent, expressive brushwork; dramatic atmosphere and light (storms, fog, sunsets); the Rückenfigur (figure seen from behind).
- **Key artists / works**: Caspar David Friedrich, *Wanderer above the Sea of Fog*; J. M. W. Turner, *Snow Storm*, *Rain, Steam and Speed*; Théodore Géricault, *The Raft of the Medusa*; Eugène Delacroix, *Liberty Leading the People* (1830); Francisco Goya, *The Third of May 1808*, the *Black Paintings*.
- **Idea**: Feeling, individuality, nature's power, nationalism, the irrational and the dark side of Enlightenment.
- **Use it today**: Rückenfigur hero shot for an outdoor, travel, or automotive campaign (viewer steps into the protagonist's place); Turner atmospheric haze as a color-grade for a weather-tech or energy brand; Delacroix's triangle-of-insurgency composition for a cause campaign.
- **Lineage**: Every "lone figure on a cliff" travel ad and Instagram hiking post; epic fantasy concept art.

## Realism

- **When / where**: France, roughly 1840s-1880s; parallel movements in Russia (the Peredvizhniki, "Wanderers") and elsewhere.
- **Visual DNA**: Ordinary people at life size; earthy, unidealized palette; flat, unheroic composition; direct observation.
- **Key artists / works**: Gustave Courbet, *The Stone Breakers*, *A Burial at Ornans*; Jean-François Millet, *The Gleaners* (1857); Honoré Daumier's lithographic caricatures; Rosa Bonheur, *The Horse Fair*; Ilya Repin, *Barge Haulers on the Volga*.
- **Idea**: "Show me an angel and I'll paint one" (attributed to Courbet): only the visible, present world. Social and political conscience about labor and class.
- **Use it today**: Documentary, unretouched portraiture for a trade, workwear, or agriculture brand (real workers, real hands); Millet's low horizon and bowed figures for a food-provenance campaign.
- **Lineage**: Social realist photography, "real people not models" advertising (Dove Real Beauty is a cousin), and the documentary-style UGC aesthetic.

## Impressionism

- **When / where**: Paris, first group exhibition 1874; active roughly 1860s-1880s.
- **Visual DNA**: Broken, visible brushstrokes; plein-air painting of fleeting light and atmosphere; colored shadows (violet, blue) and high-key palette; casual, cropped compositions influenced by photography and Japanese prints.
- **Key artists / works**: Claude Monet, *Impression, Sunrise* (the painting that named the movement), *Water Lilies*; Pierre-Auguste Renoir, *Bal du moulin de la Galette*; Edgar Degas dancers; Berthe Morisot; Mary Cassatt; Gustave Caillebotte, *Paris Street; Rainy Day*.
- **Idea**: Modern life and perception: painting what the eye actually receives in a moment, in the new leisure spaces of a changing city.
- **Use it today**: A "light study" series for a sunscreen, eyewear, or rosé brand: the same scene at five times of day (Monet's series logic); soft-focus, high-key color grading; cropped, off-center candid framing for lifestyle content.
- **Lineage**: Golden-hour photography culture, Instagram filters favoring high-key warmth, and the "series" approach to campaign content.

## Post-Impressionism

- **When / where**: France, roughly 1886-1905. The term was coined by Roger Fry for his 1910 London exhibition.
- **Visual DNA**:
  - Seurat: pointillism (divisionism), optical color mixing, rigid geometric order.
  - Van Gogh: impasto, directional rhythmic stroke, expressive complementary color.
  - Cézanne: constructive brushwork, multiple viewpoints, nature reduced to cylinder, sphere, and cone.
  - Gauguin and the Nabis: flat cloisonné color areas outlined in dark contour, symbolic color.
- **Key artists / works**: Georges Seurat, *A Sunday on La Grande Jatte*; Vincent van Gogh, *The Starry Night* (MoMA); Paul Cézanne, *Mont Sainte-Victoire* series; Paul Gauguin (whose Tahitian work is now read critically for its colonial gaze); Henri de Toulouse-Lautrec.
- **Idea**: Beyond optical impression: structure (Cézanne), science (Seurat), emotion (Van Gogh), symbol (Gauguin).
- **Use it today**: Pointillist dot field as a halftone or particle effect in 3D motion for a tech brand (data as dots resolving into an image); Van Gogh's swirling directional stroke as a flow-field generative texture; Cézanne's geometric simplification as a low-poly avatar style.
- **Lineage**: Halftone printing and LED displays share Seurat's optical mixing; "immersive Van Gogh" projection shows are a 2020s mass phenomenon; Cézanne leads directly to Cubism.

## Symbolism (brief)

- **When / where**: France, Belgium, and beyond, roughly 1880s-1900s.
- **Visual DNA**: Dreamlike, mythological subjects; muted or jewel-like palettes; femme fatale and mysticism.
- **Key artists**: Gustave Moreau, Odilon Redon, Fernand Khnopff, Arnold Böcklin (*Isle of the Dead*).
- **Use it today**: Mood boards for fragrance and luxury storytelling where the product is a talisman.

# PART 3: MODERNISMS (1890s-1950s)

## Art Nouveau (and its regional names)

- **When / where**: Roughly 1890-1910. Art Nouveau (France, Belgium), Jugendstil (Germany), Secession (Vienna), Modernisme (Catalonia), Stile Liberty (Italy).
- **Visual DNA**:
  - Whiplash line: long, sinuous, organic curves derived from plants, hair, insects.
  - Unified decoration across architecture, furniture, glass, jewelry, and poster (Gesamtkunstwerk).
  - Flat, decorative color with outlined forms (influenced by ukiyo-e); circular "halo" framing of figures.
  - New industrial materials (cast iron, glass) bent into natural forms.
- **Key artists / works**: Alphonse Mucha, posters for Sarah Bernhardt (*Gismonda*); Victor Horta, Hôtel Tassel (Brussels); Hector Guimard, Paris Métro entrances; Antoni Gaudí, Casa Batlló and Sagrada Família; Gustav Klimt, *The Kiss*; Louis Comfort Tiffany glass; René Lalique jewelry.
- **Idea**: Reconcile art with industry and nature; reject historicist revival; beauty in everyday objects.
- **Use it today**: Whiplash-line ornamental frames for a botanical spirits, tea, or tarot brand; Mucha-style halo portrait as a creator or influencer avatar template; organic 3D architecture for a skincare brand's virtual store.
- **Lineage**: 1960s psychedelic posters revived Mucha and Beardsley directly; "cottagecore" and witchy packaging; Klimt's gold reappears in luxury and wedding branding.

## Expressionism

- **When / where**: Germany and Austria, roughly 1905-1920s. Die Brücke (Dresden, 1905: Kirchner, Heckel, Schmidt-Rottluff); Der Blaue Reiter (Munich, 1911: Kandinsky, Franz Marc); Vienna (Schiele, Kokoschka); precursor Edvard Munch (Norway).
- **Visual DNA**: Distorted form and anti-naturalistic, clashing color; jagged, raw woodcut line; compressed, unstable space; emotional intensity over appearance.
- **Key artists / works**: Edvard Munch, *The Scream* (1893); Ernst Ludwig Kirchner, Berlin street scenes; Franz Marc, *Blue Horse*; Egon Schiele self-portraits; Käthe Kollwitz prints; German Expressionist film (*The Cabinet of Dr. Caligari*, 1920).
- **Idea**: Inner experience projected outward; anxiety of urban modernity and war; "primitivism" (borrowing from African and Oceanic art, now critiqued).
- **Use it today**: Woodcut texture and acid palette for a mental-health awareness campaign or a horror-film key art; Caligari's skewed sets as a stylized 3D environment for a gaming or Halloween activation.
- **Lineage**: Neo-Expressionism (1980s: Basquiat, Baselitz); punk graphics; *The Scream* is a top-tier meme and emoji (the "face screaming in fear" emoji).

## Cubism

- **When / where**: Paris, roughly 1907-1920s. Analytic (c. 1909-12) and Synthetic (c. 1912 onward).
- **Visual DNA**:
  - Multiple simultaneous viewpoints fractured into faceted planes.
  - Analytic: near-monochrome browns and grays, dense shallow space.
  - Synthetic: collage and papier collé (pasted paper, newsprint, wallpaper), brighter flat shapes, lettering inside the picture.
  - Flattening of the picture plane; form reduced to geometric planes (after Cézanne).
- **Key artists / works**: Pablo Picasso, *Les Demoiselles d'Avignon* (1907, MoMA), *Still Life with Chair Caning* (first collage); Georges Braque, *Violin and Candlestick*; Juan Gris; Sonia and Robert Delaunay (Orphism, color circles). Picasso's debt to African masks is now framed as appropriation of objects taken under colonialism.
- **Idea**: Painting as a construction of knowledge about an object, not a window. Time and multiple perspectives in one image.
- **Use it today**: Multi-angle product composite: one hero image combining front, side, and top views; collage system for a festival or editorial brand; faceted low-poly 3D avatar.
- **Lineage**: David Hockney's photo "joiners" (1980s) descend from Cubist simultaneity; collage social graphics; Picasso-style "cubist filter" AI effects.

## Futurism

- **When / where**: Italy, launched by F. T. Marinetti's manifesto published in *Le Figaro* in 1909; active to the 1940s; Russian Cubo-Futurism parallel.
- **Visual DNA**: Lines of force, repeated overlapping forms showing motion (influenced by chronophotography); speed, machines, and the city; fragmented, dynamic diagonals; typographic "words in freedom" (parole in libertà).
- **Key artists / works**: Umberto Boccioni, *Unique Forms of Continuity in Space* (1913); Giacomo Balla, *Dynamism of a Dog on a Leash*; Gino Severini; Natalia Goncharova (Russia).
- **Idea**: Glorify speed, technology, youth, and violence; destroy museums and the past. Its embrace of war and later ties to Fascism are part of the record.
- **Use it today**: Motion-trail and time-slice photography for an athletic shoe or EV launch; overlapping ghosted frames in 3D renders to show speed; parole-in-libertà typography for loud, kinetic social video.
- **Lineage**: Speed lines in comics and anime; motion-blur sports ads; kinetic typography (file 04).

## Dada

- **When / where**: Cabaret Voltaire, Zurich, 1916 (Hugo Ball, Emmy Hennings, Tristan Tzara, Jean Arp, Sophie Taeuber-Arp); then Berlin, Cologne, Hanover, Paris, New York.
- **Visual DNA**: Photomontage and collage from mass media; chance operations; readymades (found objects declared art); anarchic, mixed typography; absurdist performance and sound poetry.
- **Key artists / works**: Marcel Duchamp, *Fountain* (1917, a signed urinal); Hannah Höch, *Cut with the Kitchen Knife Dada through the Last Weimar Beer-Belly Cultural Epoch of Germany*; Raoul Hausmann; John Heartfield's anti-Nazi photomontages; Kurt Schwitters, *Merz* collages.
- **Idea**: Anti-art in response to the mechanized slaughter of WWI: if reason led to this, embrace nonsense. Questions what art is and who decides.
- **Use it today**: Photomontage for a satirical or activist campaign; readymade logic in product marketing ("this ordinary object, framed as art"); randomized, rule-based content generation (chance as method) for social.
- **Lineage**: Punk collage graphics, meme culture, absurdist "shitposting" brand accounts, and the conceptual art of the 1960s all descend from Dada.

## Surrealism

- **When / where**: Paris, from André Breton's first *Manifesto of Surrealism* (1924); international through the 1940s, including Mexico, the Caribbean, Egypt, and Japan.
- **Visual DNA**:
  - Veristic Surrealism: hyper-precise rendering of impossible scenes (Dalí, Magritte).
  - Automatism: unplanned, biomorphic forms (Miró, Masson).
  - Techniques: frottage, decalcomania, exquisite corpse (collaborative drawing), dislocated scale, uncanny juxtaposition.
- **Key artists / works**: Salvador Dalí, *The Persistence of Memory* (1931, MoMA); René Magritte, *The Treachery of Images* ("Ceci n'est pas une pipe"); Max Ernst; Meret Oppenheim, *Object* (fur-covered teacup, 1936); Leonora Carrington; Remedios Varo; Wifredo Lam (Cuba); Kay Sage; Dorothea Tanning.
- **Idea**: Freud-influenced liberation of the unconscious, dreams, and desire; revolution of the mind.
- **Use it today**: The biggest single source for "scroll-stopping" social and CGI ads: oversized products in landscapes, melting or soft hard objects, impossible materials (fur, liquid, glass swaps); exquisite-corpse avatar generators that combine parts.
- **Lineage**: Fake out-of-home (FOOH) CGI ads, the "surreal product shot" trend, Jacquemus-style giant-object campaigns, and much AI-image aesthetics all descend from Surrealism.

## Suprematism and Constructivism (Russia)

- **When / where**: Russia and the early Soviet Union, roughly 1913-1930s. Malevich's Suprematism (from 1915); Constructivism around Tatlin, Rodchenko, Stepanova, Popova, Lissitzky; schools such as VKhUTEMAS.
- **Visual DNA**: Pure geometric forms (square, circle, bar) floating on white; dynamic diagonals; red, black, and white; photomontage; sans-serif type as image; industrial materials (wood, metal, glass) in spatial constructions.
- **Key artists / works**: Kazimir Malevich, *Black Square* (1915); Vladimir Tatlin, model for the *Monument to the Third International*; El Lissitzky, *Beat the Whites with the Red Wedge*, Proun works; Alexander Rodchenko photomontage and steep-angle photography; Varvara Stepanova textiles; Lyubov Popova.
- **Idea**: Art in service of a new society: the artist as engineer, art into life, production art. Suprematism sought "the supremacy of pure feeling" through non-objective form.
- **Use it today**: Red-black-white diagonal layouts for an activist or challenger brand; Rodchenko's worm's-eye and bird's-eye angles for athletic or architecture photography; Proun-style floating geometric volumes as a 3D brand world.
- **Lineage**: Franz Ferdinand album covers; much protest-poster graphics; Barbara Kruger's red bars; the "bold diagonals and stark sans" look of many fitness brands (file 04 covers graphic Constructivism in detail).

## De Stijl (Neoplasticism)

- **When / where**: Netherlands, founded 1917 by Theo van Doesburg with Piet Mondrian and others; active to the early 1930s.
- **Visual DNA**: Horizontal and vertical lines only; primary colors (red, yellow, blue) plus black, white, gray; asymmetrical balance; elements extend into space in architecture and furniture.
- **Key artists / works**: Piet Mondrian, *Composition with Red, Blue and Yellow*, *Broadway Boogie Woogie* (MoMA); Gerrit Rietveld, *Red Blue Chair*, Rietveld Schröder House (Utrecht, 1924); Bart van der Leck.
- **Idea**: Universal harmony through pure abstraction; a utopian visual language for a world rebuilt after WWI.
- **Use it today**: Mondrian-grid bento layouts for a dashboard or homepage (cards of different sizes balanced asymmetrically); primary palette for a children's education or toy brand; Rietveld planar construction for a 3D furniture or brand-pavilion concept.
- **Lineage**: Yves Saint Laurent's Mondrian dress (1965); bento-grid web and app UI (the 2020s "bento box" layout trend is a direct visual descendant); L'Oréal Studio Line packaging.

## Bauhaus

- **When / where**: Germany, 1919-1933: Weimar, Dessau (from 1925), Berlin; closed under Nazi pressure. Diaspora to the US (Black Mountain College, New Bauhaus Chicago) and beyond (Tel Aviv's White City).
- **Visual DNA**: Form follows function; primary geometric shapes and colors; industrial materials (tubular steel, glass); sans-serif typography, asymmetrical layout, photography as a design element; craft workshops united with industry.
- **Key figures / works**: Walter Gropius (founder; Dessau building); László Moholy-Nagy; Josef Albers (later *Interaction of Color*, *Homage to the Square*); Anni Albers (textiles); Paul Klee and Wassily Kandinsky (teaching color and form); Marianne Brandt (metalwork); Herbert Bayer (universal alphabet); Marcel Breuer (Wassily chair).
- **Idea**: Unite art, craft, and technology to design for mass production and a better everyday life.
- **Use it today**: Primary-shape logo systems (circle, square, triangle) that build into mascots and patterns; Albers' color-interaction studies to test a brand palette's relativity; Bauhaus workshop structure as an agency process model.
- **Lineage**: Modernist corporate identity, IKEA-era furniture, Apple's industrial design ethos via Dieter Rams; see file 04 for typography.

## Art Deco

- **When / where**: Named after the 1925 Paris Exposition internationale des arts décoratifs et industriels modernes; peak roughly 1920-1940; global (New York, Miami, Shanghai, Mumbai, Napier in New Zealand, Havana, Mexico City). The term "Art Deco" itself became common only in the 1960s.
- **Visual DNA**: Stepped (ziggurat) silhouettes, sunbursts, chevrons, and fan motifs; streamlining (in the 1930s); luxury materials (lacquer, chrome, ebony, shagreen, gold) and machine precision; strong vertical emphasis and symmetry.
- **Key artists / works**: Chrysler Building, New York; Tamara de Lempicka portraits; A. M. Cassandre posters (file 04); Erté fashion illustration; Jean Dunand lacquer; Mumbai's Marine Drive buildings.
- **Idea**: Glamour of modernity: speed, machine, jazz, and global trade, with motifs drawn from Egypt, Aztec, and African art and from Cubism.
- **Use it today**: Stepped symmetrical frames and gold-on-black for a cocktail bar, hotel, or casino brand; streamlined chrome 3D product renders; sunburst graphics for a New Year campaign.
- **Lineage**: Gatsby-themed parties and the 2013 film; Miami Beach pastel deco in vaporwave; "Deco revival" luxury packaging; Y2K and space-age chrome are later chapters in streamline aesthetics.

## Mexican Muralism

- **When / where**: Mexico after the Revolution, from the early 1920s under education minister José Vasconcelos; spreading to the US in the 1930s.
- **Visual DNA**: Monumental public fresco; dense, layered narrative compositions of history and labor; Indigenous and mestizo protagonists; bold modeling and strong color; Siqueiros' experiments with industrial paint, spray, and dynamic perspective.
- **Key artists / works**: Diego Rivera, *Detroit Industry Murals* (Detroit Institute of Arts, 1932-33), National Palace murals; José Clemente Orozco, Dartmouth's Baker Library murals; David Alfaro Siqueiros. Frida Kahlo (not a muralist, but in the same circle).
- **Idea**: Art for the people, on public walls, to build national identity, teach history, and advance revolutionary politics.
- **Use it today**: Large-scale mural commissions for a brand HQ, store, or neighborhood activation with local artists; "history of the product" mural storytelling in a long panoramic web scroll; Siqueiros' dynamic multi-perspective for immersive 3D rooms.
- **Lineage**: Chicano murals in the US Southwest; the WPA murals of the New Deal; today's brand-sponsored mural festivals and hand-painted wall ads.

## Harlem Renaissance

- **When / where**: Harlem, New York, roughly the 1920s to mid-1930s; part of the wider New Negro movement (Alain Locke's anthology *The New Negro*, 1925).
- **Visual DNA**: Aaron Douglas's flat silhouetted figures in concentric bands of light, mixing Art Deco, Cubism, and African sculptural form; lush, modern portrait photography; jazz rhythm in repeated forms.
- **Key artists / works**: Aaron Douglas, *Aspects of Negro Life* (New York Public Library, Schomburg Center); James Van Der Zee photographs; Augusta Savage, *The Harp*; Archibald Motley's jazz scenes; Lois Mailou Jones; later Jacob Lawrence, *The Migration Series* (1940-41).
- **Idea**: Self-definition of Black modern identity: dignity, history, and cultural pride against racism; the arts as civil-rights strategy.
- **Use it today**: Douglas's silhouette-and-radiating-ring composition for a music festival or Black History Month campaign, commissioned with Black illustrators; Van Der Zee-style studio portraiture (props, painted backdrops, poise) for a fashion or heritage brand.
- **Lineage**: Kerry James Marshall, Kehinde Wiley, and Amy Sherald continue the project of Black figuration; Afrofuturism builds on it.

## Abstract Expressionism (New York School)

- **When / where**: New York, roughly mid-1940s to late 1950s.
- **Visual DNA**:
  - Action painting: gestural, all-over composition; dripped and poured paint; scale that envelops the body.
  - Color field: large stained or soft-edged fields of saturated color meant for contemplative immersion; Newman's "zips."
- **Key artists / works**: Jackson Pollock, *Autumn Rhythm (Number 30)* (Met); Willem de Kooning, *Woman* series; Mark Rothko's color-field canvases and the Rothko Chapel (Houston); Lee Krasner; Helen Frankenthaler, *Mountains and Sea* (soak-stain); Barnett Newman; Joan Mitchell; Norman Lewis.
- **Idea**: Existential authenticity; the canvas as "an arena in which to act" (critic Harold Rosenberg); the sublime and the spiritual (Rothko). Championed by critic Clement Greenberg; also enlisted as a symbol of American freedom in the Cold War.
- **Use it today**: Rothko-style soft-edged color fields as backgrounds for wellness or meditation apps; Pollock drips as a physical-paint texture for a paint, sneaker, or customization brand; Frankenthaler soak-stain as a fluid-sim reference in 3D.
- **Lineage**: Abstract "gradient mesh" backgrounds and aura gradients; fluid-art videos on social.

# PART 4: POSTWAR TO NOW

## Pop Art

- **When / where**: Britain from the mid-1950s (Independent Group); US from the early 1960s; also Japanese and Latin American Pop.
- **Visual DNA**: Imagery from advertising, comics, and celebrity; flat commercial color; Ben-Day dots and halftone; silkscreen repetition; heavy black outline.
- **Key artists / works**: Richard Hamilton, *Just what is it that makes today's homes so different, so appealing?* (1956); Andy Warhol, *Campbell's Soup Cans* (1962, MoMA), *Marilyn Diptych*; Roy Lichtenstein, *Whaam!*; Claes Oldenburg's giant soft sculptures; James Rosenquist; Marisol; Evelyne Axell; Keiichi Tanaami (Japan).
- **Idea**: Erase the line between high art and commercial culture; mass production, celebrity, and consumerism as subject (celebration, critique, or both).
- **Use it today**: Warhol grid repetition for a product-variant carousel (same can, eight colors); Ben-Day halftone overlays for a snack or soda brand; Oldenburg-scale oversized 3D products in public space.
- **Lineage**: Pop is the shared ancestor of most contemporary brand illustration, streetwear graphics, and the "brand as art" collaboration model (Warhol x Absolut is a direct case).

## Op Art

- **When / where**: Europe and the Americas, 1960s; named around MoMA's exhibition *The Responsive Eye* (1965).
- **Visual DNA**: Precise geometric patterns producing optical vibration, moiré, and illusions of motion and depth; high-contrast black and white or tuned complementary color; repetition with progressive distortion.
- **Key artists / works**: Bridget Riley, *Movement in Squares*, *Current*; Victor Vasarely, *Vega* series; Jesús Rafael Soto (Venezuela) and Carlos Cruz-Diez (Venezuela), kinetic and chromatic environments; Julio Le Parc (Argentina).
- **Idea**: The viewer's perception completes the work; scientific interest in vision; art as a universal, democratic experience.
- **Use it today**: Moiré and warped-grid motion loops for a nightclub, audio, or eyewear brand; Cruz-Diez color-interference floors as an immersive retail installation; Op pattern as an animated logo reveal.
- **Lineage**: 1960s mod fashion; distorted-grid and "warp" effects in contemporary motion design and posters; generative art.

## Minimalism

- **When / where**: New York, 1960s; Donald Judd's essay "Specific Objects" (1965) is a key text.
- **Visual DNA**: Industrial materials and fabrication (steel, plywood, fluorescent tubes, bricks); serial repetition and modular units; no composition in the traditional sense; literal space and presence.
- **Key artists / works**: Donald Judd, untitled stacks; Dan Flavin's fluorescent-light works; Carl Andre's floor pieces; Agnes Martin's pencil-grid paintings; Sol LeWitt's wall drawings; Anne Truitt; Ruth Asawa (wire sculptures, often discussed in this orbit).
- **Idea**: The object is what it is ("what you see is what you see," Frank Stella); remove illusion, metaphor, and the artist's hand.
- **Use it today**: Serial product display (the same unit repeated with exact spacing) for a retail fixture or packshot; Flavin-style colored light as an installation for a pop-up; Agnes Martin's quiet grids as a texture for a wellness brand.
- **Lineage**: Minimalist tech product photography; Apple and Aesop retail design; "less but better" (Dieter Rams) overlaps with Minimalist sensibility; see also Zen and Korean ceramics for non-Western minimalisms.

## Conceptual Art

- **When / where**: International, mid-1960s to 1970s (and ongoing).
- **Visual DNA**: The idea is the work; text, instructions, documents, photographs, and diagrams instead of crafted objects; dematerialization; systems and seriality.
- **Key artists / works**: Sol LeWitt, "Paragraphs on Conceptual Art" (1967); Joseph Kosuth, *One and Three Chairs* (1965); Yoko Ono, *Grapefruit* (instruction pieces); On Kawara, *Today* (date paintings); Lawrence Weiner's text works; John Baldessari; Adrian Piper; Hélio Oiticica and Lygia Clark (Brazil) in a related participatory vein.
- **Idea**: Question art's institutions and its object-hood; language and systems as material.
- **Use it today**: Instruction-based UGC campaigns ("do this, post it": Yoko Ono's logic for participatory social); text-only billboards that ask a question; On Kawara date-stamping as a daily content format.
- **Lineage**: Text-based Instagram art and much "manifesto" brand content; Barbara Kruger and Jenny Holzer's text works; the prompt itself as artwork in AI art.

## Land Art (Earthworks)

- **When / where**: US and UK mainly, late 1960s-1970s, with roots in ancient earthworks (Nazca lines, Serpent Mound, Stonehenge).
- **Visual DNA**: Monumental interventions in remote landscapes; earth, rock, water, and light as materials; entropy and time; documentation (photo, film) as how most people experience the work.
- **Key artists / works**: Robert Smithson, *Spiral Jetty* (Great Salt Lake, 1970); Nancy Holt, *Sun Tunnels*; Walter De Maria, *The Lightning Field* (1977); Michael Heizer, *Double Negative*; Andy Goldsworthy (ephemeral nature works); Christo and Jeanne-Claude wrappings; Ana Mendieta's *Siluetas*.
- **Idea**: Escape the gallery and the market; engage geological time, site, and ecology.
- **Use it today**: Aerial-drone "earthwork" logo reveal formed in sand, snow, or fields for an outdoor or sustainability brand (temporary, leave-no-trace); Goldsworthy-style ephemeral arrangements for a natural cosmetics campaign; light-and-time installations that change with the sun.
- **Lineage**: Drone photography aesthetics; eco-art and regenerative design branding.

## Afrofuturism

- **When / where**: Term coined by cultural critic Mark Dery in 1993 ("Black to the Future"); the practice runs back through Sun Ra's music and cosmology (from the 1950s), Octavia Butler's fiction, and Parliament-Funkadelic's mothership mythology.
- **Visual DNA**: Fusion of African and diasporic tradition (textiles, adornment, mask, architecture) with science fiction, cosmos, and advanced technology; metallic and iridescent materials; regal, otherworldly silhouettes; collage of past and future.
- **Key artists / works**: Sun Ra (*Space Is the Place*); Wangechi Mutu's collages; Ruth E. Carter's costumes and Hannah Beachler's production design for *Black Panther* (2018); Janelle Monáe's *Dirty Computer*; Cyrus Kabiru's sculptural eyewear (Kenya); Nnedi Okorafor's "Africanfuturism" (her term, centering the continent).
- **Idea**: Imagine Black futures; reclaim history and technology from narratives of loss; liberation through speculative imagination.
- **Use it today**: Afrofuturist world-building for a tech or music brand, led by Black creatives: iridescent 3D materials, crowns as interface elements, cosmic palettes; avatar systems that treat hair, adornment, and textile as high-tech.
- **Lineage**: Wider "solarpunk" and "Afro-solarpunk" aesthetics; fashion campaigns blending heritage and sci-fi.

## Postmodernism

- **When / where**: Roughly 1970s-1990s, international; Robert Venturi, Denise Scott Brown, and Steven Izenour's *Learning from Las Vegas* (1972) is a key architectural text.
- **Visual DNA**: Pastiche, appropriation, and quotation of historical styles; irony and double-coding; mixing high and low; bright, playful forms (Memphis design, file 04); photographic and text-based critique of media images.
- **Key artists / works**: Cindy Sherman, *Untitled Film Stills*; Barbara Kruger, *Untitled (Your body is a battleground)*; Jenny Holzer, *Truisms*; Sherrie Levine's re-photographs; Jeff Koons; Philip Johnson's AT&T Building (Chippendale top); Michael Graves; Charles Moore, Piazza d'Italia (New Orleans).
- **Idea**: Skepticism of grand narratives and of modernist purity; all images are already representations; identity is constructed.
- **Use it today**: Cindy Sherman role-play as a format for a brand ambassador playing many personas (also a strong avatar concept: one face, many characters); Kruger-style red-bar text-over-image for bold social statements (if the brand can stand behind the message); deliberate historical pastiche for a hotel or restaurant.
- **Lineage**: Kruger's look is the visual root of the Supreme logo (Supreme's box logo is widely recognized as derived from her work); "postmodern revival" in 2010s-2020s furniture and interiors.

## Street Art and Graffiti

- **When / where**: New York subway graffiti in the 1970s-80s; Philadelphia and Los Angeles cholo lettering; global by the 1990s-2000s (London, São Paulo, Berlin, Melbourne, Bogotá).
- **Visual DNA**: Wildstyle lettering (interlocking, arrowed, 3D letterforms), tags and throw-ups; stencil and wheatpaste; spray-paint gradients, fades, and drips; site-specific interaction with walls and city.
- **Key artists / works**: Jean-Michel Basquiat (as SAMO with Al Diaz); Keith Haring's subway chalk drawings; Lady Pink; Futura 2000; Banksy (stencil satire); Shepard Fairey (OBEY; Obama *Hope* poster, 2008); Os Gêmeos (Brazil); JR (giant paste-up portraits); Vhils (carved walls, Portugal).
- **Idea**: Reclaim public space; visibility for those excluded from institutions; tension between vandalism, voice, and market.
- **Use it today**: Commissioned murals and hand-painted ads (with local artists, paid fairly); wildstyle-inspired custom lettering for a streetwear or energy-drink brand; JR-style paste-up portraits of real customers on a store facade.
- **Lineage**: Street culture underlies streetwear branding; Haring's line is licensed widely; the "graffiti font" is a cliché to avoid unless done with writers.

## Contemporary Art (global, 1990s-now)

- **When / where**: Worldwide; biennials (Venice, São Paulo, Dakar, Sharjah, Gwangju) and art fairs.
- **Visual DNA**: No single style; recurring tools include immersive installation, appropriated materials, identity-driven figuration, spectacle at scale, and social practice.
- **Key artists / works**:
  - El Anatsui (Ghana/Nigeria): shimmering hangings of flattened bottle caps and copper wire.
  - Takashi Murakami (Japan): Superflat, merging anime, ukiyo-e flatness, and luxury (Louis Vuitton collaboration).
  - Yayoi Kusama (Japan): Infinity Mirror Rooms, polka dots.
  - Olafur Eliasson (Denmark/Iceland): *The Weather Project* (Tate Modern Turbine Hall, 2003).
  - Kerry James Marshall, Kehinde Wiley, Amy Sherald (US): Black figuration reclaiming the history of painting.
  - Yinka Shonibare (UK/Nigeria): Dutch wax fabric questioning "authentic" Africanness.
  - Ai Weiwei (China): activism and readymades.
  - Beatriz Milhazes (Brazil): layered, decorative color.
  - Do Ho Suh (Korea): translucent fabric architecture.
- **Idea**: Globalization, identity, memory, ecology, and spectacle; the museum as experience.
- **Use it today**: Kusama-style immersive rooms are the template for "Instagrammable" retail and brand pop-ups (do the thinking, not the polka dots); Anatsui's material transformation for a recycling or circular-economy campaign; Wiley's regal portrait-with-pattern backdrop for an ambassador series; Superflat for anime-adjacent brand mascots.
- **Lineage**: Experiential marketing, museum-of-ice-cream style pop-ups, and artist x luxury collaborations.

## Digital Art and Net Art

- **When / where**: Net.art in the mid-1990s (JODI, Olia Lialina, Vuk Ćosić, Heath Bunting); Rhizome founded in 1996; post-internet art in the late 2000s-2010s; NFTs and crypto art around 2021.
- **Visual DNA**: Browser and interface as medium; glitch, broken code, and default UI as aesthetic; hyperlinks and nonlinear narrative; low-res pixel, GIF loops, Web 1.0 tiled backgrounds; later, glossy 3D render and screenshot culture (post-internet).
- **Key artists / works**: Olia Lialina, *My Boyfriend Came Back from the War* (1996); JODI, *wwwwwwwww.jodi.org*; Cory Arcangel, *Super Mario Clouds*; Petra Cortright webcam videos; Rafaël Rozendaal websites; Beeple, *Everydays: The First 5000 Days* (sold at Christie's in 2021 for about $69 million).
- **Idea**: Art native to networks: participation, copying, ephemerality, and the critique of platforms.
- **Use it today**: Glitch and datamosh transitions for a music or gaming campaign; a website-as-artwork microsite with Web 1.0 references for a nostalgic product drop; GIF-loop social assets.
- **Lineage**: Y2K revival, vaporwave (built on 1980s-90s corporate and Windows imagery), "indie web" aesthetics, and much brutalist web design (file 04).

## Generative Art and AI Art

- **When / where**: Algorithmic art pioneers in the 1960s (Georg Nees, Frieder Nake, A. Michael Noll, Vera Molnár); Harold Cohen's AARON drawing program (from the early 1970s); Processing (Casey Reas and Ben Fry, 2001) opened code art to designers; GANs (mid-2010s) and diffusion models (2020s) brought AI image making to the mass market.
- **Visual DNA**:
  - Rule-based systems producing variation: plotter lines, flow fields, noise, recursion, cellular automata.
  - Long-form generative outputs (each mint unique) on platforms like Art Blocks (Tyler Hobbs, *Fidenza*).
  - AI: latent-space interpolation, morphing, hyper-detailed "too-smooth" surfaces; data-sculpture installations (Refik Anadol).
- **Key artists / works**: Vera Molnár, *(Des)Ordres*; Manfred Mohr; Harold Cohen, AARON; Casey Reas; Obvious, *Portrait of Edmond de Belamy* (sold at Christie's in 2018); Mario Klingemann; Refik Anadol, *Unsupervised* (MoMA, 2022-23); Sougwen Chung (human-robot drawing); Holly Herndon and Mat Dryhurst (consent and voice models).
- **Idea**: The artist designs the system, not the single image; authorship, chance, and control. AI art adds urgent questions of training data, consent, labor, and originality.
- **Use it today**: A rule-based generative identity (logo or pattern that varies per user, product, or data input) for a tech or music brand; AI-assisted ideation followed by human-directed finishing; data-sculpture visuals built from a client's own (permissioned) data. Advise clients on disclosure, rights to training data, and avoiding imitation of living artists' styles.
- **Lineage**: Dynamic identities (e.g., MIT Media Lab's algorithmic logo, 2011), "AI aesthetic" glossiness that audiences now recognize and sometimes reject, which in turn fuels the handmade-texture backlash of the mid-2020s.

## Challenge Prompts for the Agent

Use these to push a brief beyond the first reference:

- "You named Bauhaus. Would an Inca tocapu grid or a Kufic square script solve the same modular problem with a less obvious result?"
- "This minimalism brief: is it Judd's industrial minimal, Agnes Martin's spiritual minimal, ma's interval, or the moon jar's imperfect minimal? Pick one; they light and photograph differently."
- "You want 'luxury gold.' Byzantine gold is sacred and frontal; Rinpa gold is seasonal and asymmetrical; Deco gold is machine-age glamour; Benin brass is royal archive. Which story?"
- "This avatar reads as generic AI. Give it a historical portrait logic: Fayum frontality, Ife serenity, Mughal profile, Heian anonymity, or Sherman role-play."
- "Before borrowing this pattern, whose is it, is it living, and who should be paid or credited?"
